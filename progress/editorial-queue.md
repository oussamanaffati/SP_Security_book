## Primary homes and chapter boundaries

- **Chapter 1 — stakes, thesis, investment choices.** Show why an operator faces two different AI risks: delegated authority inside the business and faster external threats. Frame the consequences for service continuity and where an investment changes the choice. Do not repeat the implementation instructions.
- **Chapter 2 — identity, data, connectors and enforceable boundaries.** Explain what an agent can reach, how authority crosses systems, and how to prove that a boundary is enforced. Keep the source-contract and memory-scope tests here.
- **Chapter 3 — decisions under pressure.** Own response timing, decision rights during an incident, containment choices, fallback and restoration. Keep live-incident and recovery guidance here; distinguish authorization from technical proof.
- **Chapter 4 — implementation sequence and tools.** Primary home for the agent/workflow register, read/recommend/execute matrix, SOC/NOC exercise criteria and 12-month implementation roadmap. Define evidence levels here: supplier assertion/design documentation, deployed-configuration review, bounded technical test, and operational exercise or observation. They answer different questions; none proves the whole workflow.
- **Chapter 5 — executive choices, funding and oversight.** Primary home for the board decision paper and exception/acceptance record. Recommended governance model: the service owner supplies impact, tolerance and fallback evidence; the authorized service executive accepts service residual risk only within delegated authority and approved appetite; the board sets appetite, resources and challenge, not operational acceptance. Escalate when authority is absent or appetite is exceeded.

## Structural rules

Replace repeated paragraph-ending formulas about evidence, ownership, appetite and escalation with the decision consequence that matters in that passage. Revisit authority only when the decision or affected service changes. Keep a limitation only where it changes an option or confidence in the control.

Remove reader-facing process labels such as “supplied composite” and “author guidance.” Introduce a scenario naturally as illustrative once; state only the factual boundary a reader needs, such as whether a change actually occurred. Do not repeat caveats at every retelling.

Keep tables only for comparison or compact decisions. Give each tool one primary home; convert dense narration/checklist rows into short prose, reduce columns, and preserve only fields needed to decide or verify. Use varied openings and sentence lengths. Prefer concrete verbs and a clear recommendation under stated conditions. Archive the oversized historical daily log unchanged, then keep the active `progress/daily-log.md` to a compact status plus short cycle entries and an exact next action.

## Baseline weaknesses and current review

At `HEAD 38ed119` (27,482 words), the five highest-impact weaknesses were:

1. **Chapter 4’s funded-roadmap promise did not match its order.** It opened Move 4 as “A funded roadmap beats a collection of tools,” then detailed pilot expansion, routine-service handoff and fallback before placing the 12-month planning sequence at the end. The new heading is “Turn priorities into controlled operational change”; the sequence now appears before the implementation gates.
2. **The proof path risked becoming circular when production tests were unsafe.** The pilot text said “keep write authority denied and use read/draft” but did not fully specify the interim service path and escalation when that restriction could not sustain service. The current text keeps write authority denied, allows only the last verified read/draft scope or a service-preserving manual path, marks production behavior unknown, holds expansion and routes service conflict to a delegated executive or escalation. The “positive average alone is not a scale-up case” correction was already present at baseline and remains.
3. **Chapter 5 used editorial ownership language instead of reader guidance.** A representative line was “Chapter 4 owns the operational evidence gates; use those results and service-owner input to compare funding and residual exposure.” Similar Chapter 2/4 ownership assignments appeared in several decision sections. They have been removed or rewritten as direct decision guidance.
4. **Board choices converged on funding another investigation.** Examples included “Protect a bounded tranche for service mapping, supplier-supported design and a test,” “Protect a bounded tranche for supplier data-path and support-access evidence,” and “Protect a bounded review and assurance tranche to map account reach.” The revised asks now choose among migration, supplier-feature continuity, a task-bounded NOC pilot and patch readiness.

It does not select a model, approve a firewall rule or accept operational service risk,” without presenting this as a recommended governance model. Chapter 1 opened, “That responsibility is familiar. Fund work that improves those answers.” The opening/ending did not connect the central thesis to an actionable synthesis. The current text qualifies governance as a recommendation and addresses the reader’s three-or-four-priority choice; Chapter 5 closes on how exceptions must be reviewed without silently extending them. The Conclusion tells the CISO or principal architect to choose the first service path to fund, name the work that moves and act before its change window closes.
