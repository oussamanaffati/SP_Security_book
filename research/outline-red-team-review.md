# Red-team review: research base and proposed book architecture

**Review date:** 16 November 2026
**Scope:** `research/research-map.md`, `research/trend-synthesis.md`, `outline-v2.md`, and `progress/restart-research-log.md`. This is an editorial and evidence audit, not a chapter draft. The legacy manuscript and outline were not edited.

## Executive verdict

The concept has a credible, differentiated center: separate what is deployed from what is marketed; follow delegated identity and authority to the network target; and test security controls without breaking service. The six-chapter alternative provides a distinct decision sequence. The current claim ledger and qualification register bound the remaining survey/date, operator-metric and incident-attribution gaps. A constrained pilot draft is ready, provided it does not depend on sector-wide adoption/ROI, AI attribution in telecom intrusions, or unverified operator identity-to-network paths. This is not publication-level empirical validation; the sparse public record should shape the length and claims of the threat chapter rather than be filled with generic AI material.

**Confidence:** medium on the structural recommendations; medium on the audited public deployment and survey statements; low on sector prevalence, agent-specific telco incident rates, comparative control effectiveness, and causal ROI. This review is not a substitute for operator validation.

## Five most serious weaknesses, ranked by impact

1. **The architecture has ten chapters but only about six distinct executive decisions.** Chapters 1 and 2 both classify and inventory deployment; 4, 5, and 6 all explain where authority and control boundaries sit; 7 and 8 both concern proving and exercising those boundaries; 9 combines two large topics (jurisdictional obligations and supplier dependency); 10 risks restating every earlier recommendation. Readers may experience repetition before reaching the promised 12-month decision. Rebuild around six decisions, not ten trend headings.
2. **Public evidence is much stronger for bounded use and operator/vendor claims than for security outcomes.** The ledger now separates production, pilots, validation, announcements and customer-facing tools; operator-reported task and service-volume claims remain distinct from productivity, service quality and security outcomes. Airtel/Ericsson and China Mobile/ZTE percentages, prospective capacity and unitemized adoption totals are excluded from headline outcomes. These public cases do not establish safer networks, fewer incidents, better service, causal productivity, or sector-wide ROI.
3. **The remaining source limits are bounded, not erased.** The claim ledger records exact support where retrieved, source class, method gaps and use disposition. Heavy Reading’s stated July/August fieldwork and June figure notes remain unreconciled in the source; the ledger preserves both and the pilot must not depend on a reconstructed explanation. Some original pages (including TELUS and IMDA) remain unreadable and their claims are omitted or unresolved rather than reconstructed from snippets.
4. **The book risks making a plausible security model sound like an established telecom fact.** “The decisive risk is often the operational connection among identities, data, tools, suppliers and network targets” is a useful thesis, but public evidence currently establishes architectural risk mechanisms, not their frequency or dominance in operators. AgentDojo is a peer-reviewed simulated benchmark; O-RAN findings are lab/testbed research; CISA guidance is expert recommendation. Treat identity-to-target authority as the book’s analytic lens and testable hypothesis, not as a quantified leading cause of telco incidents.
5. **Operator reality is still inferred too much from announcements and public standards.** The research itself acknowledges no interviews. Public disclosures rarely expose errors, overrides, rollback, active/queued action revocation, service impact, cost, internal SOC adoption, or legal interpretations. A book promising useful operational decisions must validate these details across multiple operators and markets; otherwise checklists about fallback, NOC/SOC coordination, supplier access and board priorities may be sound but generic.

## Chapter-by-chapter red-team

### Current Chapter 1 — “The First Question Is: What Is Actually Deployed?”
- **Reader question / CISO decision:** What does “we use AI” mean in a named workflow, and what evidence threshold is required before it enters the risk register or portfolio? Decide how the operator will classify announcement, pilot, limited production and scaled production.
- **Evidence and classes:** 3GPP’s explainer is standards-body context, not prevalence data. Heavy Reading is a sponsored survey of 84 people, not 84 independent operators; 47% is self-reported autonomy (n=83), and over 74% is stated agent-deployment intention (n=82–83). DT RAN Guardian and One NZ Concierge are operator-reported deployments with operator-reported results. These support an evidence taxonomy and bounded examples, not a sector maturity estimate.
- **Weakness / overstatement:** The chapter’s opening claim that operators already use several forms of AI is supportable in a broad qualitative sense, but “use” spans analytics, ML, deterministic automation and agentic systems. The source set does not support a clean market-wide breakdown. Avoid implying that “production” means scaled, independently measured, safe, or security-relevant.
- **Overlap / disposition:** It duplicates Chapter 2’s use-case heat map. Merge the classification method with Chapter 2; keep the claim card as a reusable tool, not a full standalone chapter.
- **Generic-AI risk:** Moderate. Taxonomy can become a generic AI maturity ladder unless each example identifies operator workflow, target, authority, service consequence and measurement evidence.
- **Payoff / full chapter?** The claim card is useful, but not enough for a separate chapter. Fold into a stronger opening chapter that tests market claims against real operator workflows.

### Current Chapter 2 — “Where AI Is Changing Telecom Work”
- **Reader question / CISO decision:** Which workflows are genuinely deployed or piloted, and which should be allowed to progress based on service, authority and evidence? Decide what belongs in a local inventory and where to demand stronger proof.
- **Evidence and classes:** Operator reports include DT RAN Guardian (operator says live in production; its November 2025 launch and January 2026 “live since” statements remain unreconciled; first-month action and event-time figures are company-reported), One NZ Concierge (operator says deployed; 80% analysis-time figure lacks case count, baseline and answer-quality measure), Orange MAIA (operator says deployed to 3,000 advisers and nearly 1 million conversations/month; this is adoption/volume, not service-quality evidence), SKT recommender (commercial-network validation, not autonomous live control), Telefónica’s unitemized 500+ “AI use cases” (excluded as a prevalence count), Airtel (supplier/association case report; performance figures excluded), China Mobile/ZTE (pilot and event-specific supplier reports; percentages excluded), and Google Cloud’s separate One NZ VoLTE agent (vendor page uses both “has deployed” and “is deploying”; stage unresolved). BT is a customer-facing call-screening service with aggregate service telemetry but no efficacy denominator. Orange Cyberdefense’s Qevlar is a gradual SOC-integration announcement, not proof of telco-internal SOC adoption. TELUS source bodies remain unreadable; its figures are omitted.
- **Weakness / overstatement:** A long parade of cases can imply breadth while avoiding a consistent unit of comparison. “Nearly one million conversations” is volume, not quality or security. “80% reduction” concerns analysis time, not restoration time or service improvement. Airtel’s 69%/29%/47%/26% figures and China Mobile/ZTE percentages lack sufficient public baselines, denominators or KPI definitions; they are not comparative outcomes. Do not compare the retained source claims or derive ROI from them.
- **Overlap / disposition:** Merge with Chapter 1. Present a small number of contrasting operator cases (one bounded production control action, one advisory/workflow agent, one pilot, one customer-service assistant) and a compact status matrix; move the rest to evidence boxes/appendix or omit unless they change a decision.
- **Generic-AI risk:** Moderate-to-high if organized by model type. Keep it organized by operating workflow and allowed action, with network/service implications.
- **Payoff / full chapter?** Strong when joined to Chapter 1; weak as a second adjacent chapter. The inventory and claim card are actionable, but require a local evidence gate.

### Current Chapter 3 — “AI in the Threat Picture: What We Know and What We Do Not”
- **Reader question / CISO decision:** What is demonstrably AI-enabled in attacks against telecom, and what threat preparation is justified without an AI label? Decide which observed adversary techniques to prioritize and what attribution standard to use.
- **Evidence and classes:** Singapore’s four-operator targeting disclosure and FBI/CISA’s U.S. telecom campaign statements are readable government incident evidence; their reviewed incident accounts do not mention AI. The IMDA Indonesian-backdoor advisory remains unreadable in two official PDF routes and is omitted. Google’s GRIDTIDE account is vendor threat-intelligence evidence from an earlier direct review and reports 53 victims across sectors, not a telecom-only count; fresh retrieval failed in this pass. Google/Anthropic AI-misuse reports are provider telemetry outside a telco-specific denominator. The Canadian government bulletin is cross-sector threat guidance, not a telco incident or universal response time. ENISA’s 30 OSINT-listed incidents and 188 authority-submitted reports are distinct datasets and not comparable rates.
- **Weakness / overstatement:** The small public body of AI-attributed telco incidents does not support a standalone narrative of an AI-driven telecom threat wave. Conversely, absence of attribution is not evidence of absence. Do not turn broad AI capability reports or short attack timelines into telecom-specific risk estimates.
- **Overlap / disposition:** Keep the external-adversary question distinct from the operator’s internal agent exposure, but make it a short, forceful chapter or a major section in the opening “reality” chapter. It should not be padded to a chapter-length threat catalogue.
- **Generic-AI risk:** High if it surveys AI phishing, malware or autonomous attacks without telecom-specific evidence. Use telecom incident records first, then clearly label broader evidence as context only.
- **Payoff / full chapter?** Important to the book’s credibility and title. A full chapter is justified only if it teaches a usable threat-claim ladder and changes prioritization; otherwise integrate it into the opening chapter.

### Current Chapter 4 — “The New Internal Path: Identity, Data, Tools and Targets”
- **Reader question / CISO decision:** Which AI-enabled workflow can reach which operational target, under whose identity and authority? Decide where to inventory, constrain and test delegated authority.
- **Evidence and classes:** CISA’s 2026 agentic guidance is expert/multi-agency recommendation. AgentDojo is peer-reviewed technical evidence (97 tasks, 629 security cases, 74 simulated tools), but not a telco deployment. The IEEE O-RAN xApp study is peer-reviewed lab research with three tested attack paths and disclosed support; the Computer Networks O-RAN study is peer-reviewed research/test-case evidence, not production frequency. These support mechanisms and test questions, not prevalence or telco incident attribution.
- **Weakness / overstatement:** OSS/BSS, RAN/core, contractors and cloud are examples of potential paths, not proof that a specific agent has access to them. “The control problem follows the full chain” is a useful analytic model, not a demonstrated universal failure pattern. The outline should distinguish retrieved data, recommendations, API calls, accepted changes and downstream network state.
- **Overlap / disposition:** Merge with Chapters 5 and 6 into one telecom-specific authority-and-control-boundary chapter. Keep the identity-to-target map as the main artifact.
- **Generic-AI risk:** Medium. Prompt-injection descriptions can become generic; anchor every mechanism to a real telecom identity, interface, change workflow or data boundary, and label scenarios as illustrative when not observed.
- **Payoff / full chapter?** Strong as part of the core. The map is a valuable operator exercise; the current standalone chapter does not yet have enough operator-validated paths to sustain the broad examples.

### Current Chapter 5 — “Why Telecom Changes the Security Answer”
- **Reader question / CISO decision:** Which carrier-specific service dependencies and operating constraints change the safe design or response? Decide which domains, suppliers, fallback paths and maintenance constraints must be in scope.
- **Evidence and classes:** 3GPP/GSMA/ETSI standards and guidance, UK regulations/code, operator disclosures, and peer-reviewed O-RAN lab work provide architectural and institutional context. They are not evidence of a uniform operational condition at every carrier. The listed evidence should be mapped to concrete decisions, not assembled as a general claim that telecom is complex or critical.
- **Weakness / overstatement:** “One identity plane does not exist” and claims about roaming, lawful-intercept dependencies, maintenance windows and legacy constraints need specific sources or operator validation. Some are plausible but not established by the cited standards. Avoid asserting that every operator has the same topology or failure mode.
- **Overlap / disposition:** Do not retain as a standalone architecture survey. Thread the specific operational constraint into the relevant cases and combine the decision tools with Chapter 4’s authority map and Chapter 7/8’s control and response exercises.
- **Generic-AI risk:** High if it becomes generic “telecom is critical” background. Use concrete service chains and consequences, supported by operator interviews.
- **Payoff / full chapter?** The dependency sketch is useful but does not justify a standalone chapter unless operator evidence reveals recurring decision patterns. Fold it into the core authority/continuity chapters.

### Current Chapter 6 — “Zero Trust, IGA and AI: Useful Pieces, Not a Magic Stack”
- **Reader question / CISO decision:** Which identity lifecycle, policy and target-enforcement control changes a real telecom risk, and what is merely a combined architecture proposal? Decide which convergence claims merit a bounded test.
- **Evidence and classes:** NIST SP 800-207 is voluntary ZTA architecture guidance; SP 1800-35 is lab/example implementation, not carrier outcomes. GCOT is government policy guidance; Ericsson is vendor architecture guidance; SKT’s paper is an operator vision. TM Forum’s IPOE/access-management Catalysts are proof-of-concept demonstrations. Omada’s Telenor IGA case is vendor-reported procurement/implementation evidence for a distinct IGA component. Protiviti is consulting commentary, not implementation evidence.
- **Weakness / overstatement:** The convergence is a useful case study in market rhetoric, but not necessarily a book-sized problem on its own. The statement that NIST examples, 3GPP and GSMA “pieces” combine should not imply a standardized integrated stack. ZTA, IGA and AI solve different problems; explain their interfaces and failure modes without presenting a formula.
- **Overlap / disposition:** Merge into Chapter 4’s authority-boundary chapter. Keep Protiviti as a case study in separating a proposed architecture from an evidenced outcome. Do not make the chapter a survey of vendor frameworks.
- **Generic-AI risk:** High. Generic ZTA/IGA explanations will dilute the telecom-specific promise unless tied to service identities, network targets, revocation and operator workflows.
- **Payoff / full chapter?** The convergence test is valuable as a section or field tool, not a full chapter by itself on current evidence.

### Current Chapter 7 — “Prove the Boundary Before You Trust It”
- **Reader question / CISO decision:** What evidence shows a prohibition is enforced at the actual target, including active sessions and queued work? Decide whether authority can be enabled, narrowed or revoked safely.
- **Evidence and classes:** CISA and NIST tool-risk material are recommendations/workshop synthesis; AgentDojo and O-RAN are bounded technical evaluations/testbeds; NetInjectBench is a preprint whose publication status and assumptions remain unresolved. These support a test strategy, not a universal efficacy rate. Public operator production-test evidence is sparse.
- **Weakness / overstatement:** “A control is only proven for the path and conditions tested” is sound assurance logic, but it needs to be framed as a principle rather than an empirically validated telecom finding. Assertions about token revocation not stopping active sessions/queued work should be demonstrated or explicitly posed as a failure mode to test, not stated as a fact about all products.
- **Overlap / disposition:** Merge with Chapter 8 into a practical assurance-and-response chapter. Keep a test-evidence record and service-safe exercise, but separate test result from operational recovery evidence.
- **Generic-AI risk:** Medium. General red-team methods are relevant only when applied to a named identity-to-target path and carrier service constraints.
- **Payoff / full chapter?** Strong if the operator can execute the evidence protocol. It merits a substantial chapter only with operator-validated exercises, target logs, stop behavior and fallback examples.

### Current Chapter 8 — “When the Alarm Is Real: SOC/NOC Decisions and Service Continuity”
- **Reader question / CISO decision:** Who can decide and contain when the SOC, NOC, IAM and supplier hold different evidence or control, and how is service restored? Decide command authority, service-specific thresholds and fallback.
- **Evidence and classes:** Official incident disclosures and national guidance establish threat/response realities, but rarely document AI-agent response chains. Operator continuity plans and interviews are needed. The outline currently cites examples only generally; it does not yet show a verified case of an AI containment action impairing service or a validated multi-team decision model.
- **Weakness / overstatement:** Risk of presenting a plausible scenario as a documented operator failure. Do not claim that containment “removes” visibility or that response objectives must take a particular form without operator evidence; present these as hypotheses and test questions.
- **Overlap / disposition:** Combine with Chapter 7, but retain a distinct second half on decision authority and recovery. The difference is important: testing asks “does the control work?”; response asks “can the operator act and restore safely under pressure?”
- **Generic-AI risk:** Moderate-to-high if built around generic incident command. Make telecom-specific service consequences, change windows, supplier repair access, NOC view and alternate communications evidence-led.
- **Payoff / full chapter?** Could justify a full chapter after cross-operator validation. Without it, offer a concise scenario and exercise template rather than generalized claims.

### Current Chapter 9 — “Rules, Suppliers and Sovereignty”
- **Reader question / CISO decision:** Which obligations apply to this operator in each market, and which supplier/model changes could affect service, evidence or exit? Decide legal applicability, contract controls and continuity requirements.
- **Evidence and classes:** UK Electronic Communications Security Measures Regulations are binding, while the Code of Practice is guidance; NIS2, GDPR and the EU AI Act are binding instruments with role/scope-specific application; CIRCIA’s proposed reporting duties are not current blanket obligations before its final rule takes effect; NIST/GSMA/3GPP/GCOT materials have different non-statutory statuses. FCC 25-81 rescinded the earlier cybersecurity declaratory ruling and withdrew its NPRM. Supplier examples demonstrate architectures/dependencies, not comparative resilience or sovereignty outcomes.
- **Weakness / overstatement:** Law and supplier concentration are each large, changing topics. Combining them risks shallow legal summaries and speculative sovereignty claims. The outline’s phrase “TRAI proposals where applicable” needs a specific current proposal and jurisdictional status or should be removed. Regulations must be checked at manuscript publication and not generalized across markets.
- **Overlap / disposition:** Keep as one shorter “obligations and dependencies” chapter only if the two are linked by concrete operator decisions; otherwise treat jurisdictional law as a compact reference and integrate supplier failure/exit into Chapters 4 and 5. Do not claim supplier location alone establishes sovereignty or control.
- **Generic-AI risk:** High if it becomes generic AI regulation or data-localization commentary. The value is the operator’s service, role, reporting and change-control implications.
- **Payoff / full chapter?** Useful as a dated decision map, but only with legal review across named markets and a clear scope. Current evidence supports a structured map, not global legal advice.

### Current Chapter 10 — “The CISO’s Reality-Based Agenda”
- **Reader question / CISO decision:** Which three or four operator priorities should be funded or tested over the next 12 months, by whom, with what evidence gate and stop condition? Decide what the CEO/board should resource and oversee.
- **Evidence and classes:** This chapter is a synthesis of preceding evidence, not a new empirical claim. Vendor cases and surveys can suggest questions but cannot set a sector-wide investment order. The concept correctly says local cost and change-window evidence must decide.
- **Weakness / overstatement:** “Choose three or four priorities” is the reader promise, not evidence that every operator should select the same list. Do not turn the final chapter into a generic governance checklist or imply board visibility itself constitutes risk acceptance. Separate management ownership/acceptance within delegated authority from board oversight of appetite, resources and assurance.
- **Overlap / disposition:** Keep as the short decision-conversion finale. Move repeated tools and metrics to earlier chapters; synthesize, do not introduce a new catalogue.
- **Generic-AI risk:** High unless priorities are visibly constrained by service, network dependency, workforce, supplier and market context.
- **Payoff / full chapter?** Strong as a concise closing chapter if it produces a decision brief with owners, evidence gates, cost, dependency, residual risk and triggers; it need not be long.

## Revised table of contents: six decision chapters

1. **What Is Real in the Operator Estate?** Establish an evidence vocabulary and compare a small set of operator workflows across production, pilot, validation, vendor claim and intent. Include the claim card and a one-page status map. The reader decides what to believe and what to inventory.
2. **Telecom Threats Without the AI Hype** Start from verified telecom intrusion activity, then assess what public evidence does and does not establish about AI contribution. Include a threat-claim ladder and explain why the absence of AI attribution neither diminishes the incidents nor proves non-use. The reader decides which observed risks merit priority now.
3. **Follow Authority to the Network Target** Map the requester/identity, delegated credentials, data, connector, API, target enforcement and downstream service effect. Explain where Zero Trust, IGA and AI each fit, and where the Protiviti convergence remains a proposal. Use telecom-specific service identities and network domains; label lab and benchmark evidence. The reader decides which paths require restrictions or tests.
4. **Prove Safe Authority and Service-Safe Response** Combine control assurance with SOC/NOC decision-making and recovery. Test target-side allow/deny, logs, existing sessions, queued work, revoke/stop, rollback, alternate visibility and staffed fallback. Keep architecture-test evidence separate from exercise and production evidence. The reader decides whether to enable, narrow, revoke or defer authority and who leads under pressure.
5. **Obligations, Suppliers and Exit Conditions** Build a dated, jurisdiction-specific legal/standards map and connect it to model, cloud, connector, support-access, update, outage and exit dependencies. Distinguish binding law from code/guidance/standards and vendor recommendations. The reader decides which supplier and regulatory conditions need contractual or operational action.
6. **The Next 12 Months: A Defensible Operator Agenda** Convert local evidence into three or four priorities with accountable owners, service outcomes, cost/capacity, dependencies, evidence gates, fallback, residual risk, and change triggers. Distinguish board oversight from management execution and risk acceptance. The reader leaves with a board/CEO-ready decision brief.

This is a six-chapter decision sequence, not a commitment to six equal-length chapters. Chapter 2 may be shorter if primary evidence remains sparse. A compact appendix can hold the claim-card template, legal-status matrix and interview/test questions; it should not become a second book of controls.

## Narrative arc

The executive begins with a market claim and learns to ask, “What is deployed, where, and what did anyone actually measure?” The book then refuses the easy AI-threat story: telecom intrusions are already serious, while direct AI attribution remains limited. With that distinction established, the reader follows authority through the operator’s real identity, tool and network boundaries. The question shifts from model trust to target-side control and service-safe response. Only then do jurisdiction and suppliers enter as concrete constraints on operation and exit. The ending turns the evidence into a small, locally defensible 12-month agenda. The progression is: **separate signal from promotion → understand exposure → prove control → preserve service → decide and fund**.

## Prioritized operator-validation questions

### 1 — NOC/SOC (highest priority)
- Which deployed or trial AI/agent workflows can read, recommend, create a ticket, change configuration, or execute an action? Who is the accountable service owner for each?
- What exactly happens when the SOC requests isolation but the NOC sees an active service-impacting change? Who has authority to pause, continue, or roll back?
- Can teams correlate the initiating identity/request to the connector call and the target’s final state? Which logs are missing, delayed, supplier-held or non-repudiable?
- After disablement or credential revocation, what happens to active sessions, delegated tokens, scheduled jobs and queued actions? Has this been tested against the actual target?
- What failures, overrides, false positives, wrong recommendations and recovery actions are measured? What is the denominator and who reviews it?

### 2 — Network engineering / architecture
- Which OSS/BSS, RAN, core, transport, cloud/edge and service-management systems are actually connected to an AI assistant, agent, model endpoint or supplier connector? What is observed versus confirmed?
- Which API or configuration writes can the workflow perform, under which human/non-human identity, on which network elements and inside what guardrails?
- Which network domains have independent target-side authorization and audit, and where does a broker/connector become the effective control point?
- What are the service dependencies, maintenance windows, rollback semantics and safe-state behavior if model, telemetry, API or orchestration is unavailable or wrong?
- Which claims about roaming, lawful intercept, emergency calling, shared infrastructure or legacy dependencies are operator-specific, and which can be substantiated with a real service map?

### 3 — Identity/security
- Is there an inventory of human, service, workload, agent, vendor and delegated identities, with accountable owners and effective permissions rather than just product registrations?
- How are credentials issued, scoped, rotated, reviewed and revoked? Do token revocation and session termination reach every downstream system?
- Are policy decisions enforced at the network target, or only by the assistant/UI/connector? Have prohibited writes and privilege escalation paths been tested?
- How are prompt/data injection, untrusted tickets/runbooks, model changes, connector changes and tool-schema changes evaluated in the real workflow?
- What evidence links identity governance and Zero Trust controls to a measured reduction in exposure or control failure, rather than policy completion or maturity score?

### 4 — Service continuity and resilience
- Which service outcomes must not degrade during AI failure, containment, supplier outage or control-plane isolation? Who sets and accepts the service-specific thresholds?
- What minimum NOC visibility, break-glass access, manual procedure, alternate communications and supplier support must remain available during containment?
- Has a realistic exercise tested a bad agent recommendation/action while telemetry is incomplete and a concurrent service incident is in progress? What was the recovery time and what was not tested?
- What are the stop conditions, rollback triggers, restore criteria and evidence that the system returned to a known-good state?
- Which recovery dependencies are shared with the AI service itself, creating a common-mode failure?

### 5 — Suppliers / procurement
- Which model providers, cloud services, embedding/search components, connectors, telemetry, support staff and subprocessors can see data or exercise access? Where is each control enforced?
- Which supplier changes (model, weights, prompt, connector, tool schema, hosting, support route, telemetry retention) trigger notice, testing, approval or rollback?
- What are the service-level commitments, incident-notification terms, evidence access, audit rights, subcontractor controls, data deletion, and safe exit/portability conditions?
- Can the operator continue safe service if a provider is unavailable, withdraws a model, changes behavior, or cannot support incident response? What has been tested, not merely promised?
- Which claimed savings include integration, review/rework, compute, licenses, training, staffing, assurance and exit costs?

### 6 — Legal, privacy and compliance
- For each relevant jurisdiction, which entity/service and operator role is in scope under the law actually in force today? Which duties are binding versus codes, standards, consultations or proposed rules?
- What data categories and purposes are processed by each workflow, and who is controller/processor or equivalent? What retention, access, transfer and incident-reporting obligations apply?
- Does any system meet the statutory criteria for a regulated/high-risk use based on intended purpose and role, rather than critical-infrastructure status alone?
- Which incident-reporting clocks and thresholds apply across telecom, privacy, financial, national-security and critical-infrastructure regimes, and who coordinates conflicting requirements?
- Which contract terms, regulator interpretations or legal conclusions require counsel confirmation before the book presents them as general guidance?

## Recommendation

**Ready for a constrained pilot draft; not ready for publication-level generalization.** The six-chapter sequence removes the duplicated decision jobs, the source ledger now distinguishes deployment stage from operator/vendor-reported outcomes, and the Heavy Reading/ENISA discrepancies are bounded as unresolved rather than silently reconciled. Unsupported operator metrics are excluded or clearly qualified; readable primary telecom incident disclosures do not attribute AI, and the inaccessible IMDA advisory is omitted. The pilot must not depend on sector-wide adoption/ROI, AI-attributed telecom attacks, or unverified operator identity-to-network paths. Use technical studies and guidance for mechanisms, mark scenarios and local validation questions explicitly, and keep Chapter 2 short if the evidence remains sparse. Operator interviews and local measurements remain necessary before making broad empirical claims or publication-level generalizations, but they are not blockers to this constrained pilot.
