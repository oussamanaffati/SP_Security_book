# AI security in telecommunications: research map

**Research reset:** 16 November 2026. This file begins a new evidence base for *AI Security for CISOs in Telecommunications: Reality from Hype*. The earlier manuscript, outline and chapter research notes remain intact but are not authority for this phase. Nothing here is a chapter draft.

## Method and evidence rules

Searches covered operator disclosures, operator/vendor deployment announcements, threat-intelligence reports, telecom and standards bodies, regulators, consulting/analyst publications, and independent technical research. Primary sources were preferred for exact claims; secondary reporting was used to find or cross-check a source, not to substitute for it. The Protiviti article and SKT source were read in a real browser. Several sources were blocked by Cloudflare or returned 403 (including Check Point, Ericsson, some GSMA pages and TELUS); where official indexed text, a mirrored company release or an official secondary case page was used, the limitation is recorded at that source. Gated material remains unresolved rather than being treated as verified.

Classify each item before using it:

- **Observed incident/disclosure:** a named authority or operator reports an event; does not imply AI use unless stated.
- **Operator-reported deployment/result:** evidence that an operator says a system is in production; reported metrics remain company claims absent independent measurement.
- **Vendor-reported deployment/result:** a supplier describes a customer deployment or benefit; confirm operator status and distinguish plans from production.
- **Technical measurement:** a bounded experiment, benchmark or test; its conditions and environment define what it establishes.
- **Survey:** respondent opinion, reported adoption or intention, not necessarily measured use or performance.
- **Product telemetry:** observations from the publisher’s installed base; selection, definitions and denominator matter.
- **Forecast/market estimate:** a scenario, not an outcome.
- **Recommendation/standard/regulation:** separate voluntary guidance, consensus standards, proposals and binding duties by jurisdiction.
- **Consulting/vendor opinion:** useful evidence of the market narrative, not proof of implementation or effectiveness.

Confidence describes the claim, not the publisher’s reputation: **high** means direct, scoped primary evidence; **medium** means relevant evidence with material sampling or attribution limits; **low** means a proposal, single unreplicated benchmark, missing denominator or unsupported assertion. No survey percentage is treated as an operator-wide adoption rate unless the sampling frame and denominator justify that interpretation.

For every statistic retain the measured period, geography, unit, denominator/sample, source incentives, and what it does not establish. Do not compare vendor telemetry, surveys, forecasts and operator case studies as if they shared a denominator. A missing public report is not proof that an event did not happen.

## Reference article audit: Protiviti, 26 September 2025

[“Zero Trust, IGA and AI in Next-Gen Telecom Networks: CISOs’ Convergence Approach”](https://blog.protiviti.com/2025/09/26/zero-trust-iga-and-ai-in-next-gen-telecom-networks-cisos-convergence-approach/) is useful as a reader-experience specimen: it starts with recognizable telecom systems and roles, explains three security ideas in accessible language, and organizes them around a CISO’s problem. It is a Protiviti consulting blog by a managing director in Security & Privacy. That commercial context is relevant; it is not, by itself, evidence that a claim is false.

| Article claim | Trace and assessment |
|---|---|
| Telecom attacks rose **94%** year over year to **2,664 attacks per organization per week** in Q1 2025. | The Protiviti article calls this a survey result and describes telecom as the **third-highest percentage increase**. The original Check Point Research [Q1 2025 Global Cyber Attack Report](https://blog.checkpoint.com/research/q1-2025-global-cyber-attack-report-from-check-point-software-an-almost-50-surge-in-cyber-threats-worldwide-with-a-rise-of-126-in-ransomware-attacks/) reports Check Point ThreatCloud AI product telemetry, not a respondent survey. It says telecom had the **highest year-over-year percentage increase** (94%); telecom ranked third by weekly attack volume (2,664), after education (4,484) and government (2,678). The report describes telemetry across about 150,000 connected networks and millions of endpoints overall, but does not disclose the telecom-only organization count, sampling frame or exact event definition. The 2,664 is a vendor-reported weekly average per protected telecom organization, not a census of successful incidents or a direct measure of all attacks against telecom operators. The article conflates the volume rank with the percentage-increase rank and omits denominator/evidence-class caveats. |
| “FCC cybersecurity rules” require continuous monitoring and encryption. | The article gives no rule citation. The FCC’s January 2025 [FCC 25-9](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/FCC-25-9A1.pdf) included a proposed rulemaking; the Commission later [rescinded the declaratory ruling and withdrew the NPRM](https://www.fcc.gov/document/fcc-corrects-course-outlines-improved-cybersecurity-measures-0) in FCC 25-81, adopted 20 November and released 21 November 2025. Existing FCC obligations, including CPNI safeguards and breach-notification rules, are distinct. The article’s broad “require” wording is not adequately sourced and should not be repeated as a current blanket rule. |
| NIST Zero Trust guidance calls for real-time analytics and integration across legacy and modern systems. | The article’s hyperlink on “NIST zero-trust guidelines” resolves to NIST’s [post-quantum cryptography standards announcement](https://www.nist.gov/news-events/news/2024/08/nist-releases-first-3-finalized-post-quantum-encryption-standards), not to Zero Trust guidance. The relevant reference is [NIST SP 800-207](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/207/final) (August 2020), an architecture publication, not telecom regulation. It describes logical components and access principles; it does not make “real-time analytics” a universal compliance requirement. The article does not map its wording to a supporting NIST passage. |
| GDPR requires “full visibility” into data flows. | The [GDPR text](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj/eng) requires, among other things, records of processing (Article 30) and risk-appropriate security measures (Article 32). “Full visibility” is an interpretation, not the text of a blanket security duty. |
| “CISA reporting mandates” require rapid incident response. | The article does not identify the mandate or jurisdiction. CIRCIA’s 2024 notice was a proposed rule; CISA’s [current CIRCIA page](https://www.cisa.gov/topics/cyber-threats-and-advisories/information-sharing/cyber-incident-reporting-critical-infrastructure-act-2022-circia) described rulemaking as ongoing in 2026, including stakeholder town halls. Its covered-entity reporting requirements are not a generic CISA mandate for all telecom companies. Other federal, state and sector obligations must be assessed separately. |
| “Some forward-thinking telecom CISOs” use the combined approach; telecom leaders have spent a year incorporating Zero Trust into 5G/6G “in line with NIST.” | The article cites no source for the adoption assertion. Related work does exist: GCOT’s 2026 [6G Security and Resilience Principles](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/global-coalition-on-telecoms-security-and-resilience-principles-for-6g/gcot-security-and-resilience-principles-for-6g) recommend function-level security consistent with Zero Trust; Ericsson published vendor guidance for mobile-network ZTA; SK Telecom’s 2026 6G white paper presents a future-network Zero Trust vision. TM Forum’s 2026 Catalysts now describe proof-of-concept agent-governance and access-management designs [M9, M10]; Omada’s Telenor case is a separate IGA procurement example [M11]. These are a government recommendation, vendor architecture guidance, operator strategy paper, Catalyst demos and vendor case study—not evidence of widespread production deployment or measured security outcomes. |
| ZTNA, microsegmentation and IoT least privilege are being used across telecom functions; IGA “aligns perfectly” with Zero Trust. | Plausible design recommendations, but the article supplies no operator cases, penetration, baseline or outcomes. “Aligns perfectly” is promotional language, not an evidence claim. Telecom service identities, network functions, contractors, OSS/BSS and customer identities have different control points; an enterprise ZTNA design is not automatically a network-control design. |
| AI/ML role mining, just-in-time access, automated recertification, AI SOC triage, SOAR containment and self-healing networks are current innovations. | These are capability descriptions without named systems, deployment status, denominators or measured results. Treat them as market possibilities until an operator-specific production source establishes scope and outcome. |
| An “identity governance score” is a progress benchmark; AI continually adjusts Zero Trust risk thresholds. | The article defines neither measure nor source. OpenText’s vendor [IGA Buyer’s Guide](https://opentext.com/media/white-paper/iga-buyers-guide-wp-en.pdf) uses an “overall identity governance score,” illustrating a vendor-specific maturity aid; this does not establish a cross-vendor or industry metric or show that Protiviti derived its phrase from that guide. Dynamic risk scoring is a design proposal, not evidence that telecom operators safely operate this integrated loop. |

**Audit conclusion:** the article combines useful telecom context with one traceable vendor statistic, broad regulatory shorthand and a proposed architecture. Its central “AI + Zero Trust + IGA” diagram—IGA governs requests, Zero Trust enforces, AI adjusts risk—should be presented as a design hypothesis to test, not a demonstrated operator outcome. Later TM Forum Catalysts show related agent-governance and access-management prototypes, but not measured production control loops; Telenor’s vendor-reported IGA case is a separate component example. The key test is whether identity lifecycle, policy decision, target enforcement, downstream revocation, logs and service fallback actually join in a deployed operator workflow.
