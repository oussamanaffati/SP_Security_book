# Chapter 3 research notes — 13 November 2026

## Search window and question

This cycle searched the previous 12 months (13 November 2025–13 November 2026), plus 2025 operator incident records that remain within the requested research period. The question was how adversary activity can outrun detection, decision-making and safe action, especially when a provider must preserve service continuity. The chapter distinguishes threat speed from response latency and avoids claiming that all attacks use AI.

## Sources used

1. **Canadian Centre for Cyber Security, “Cyber threat bulletin: Agentic artificial intelligence is accelerating cyber attacks” (date modified 26 October 2026).** Primary government guidance. It states that attacks are not yet fully autonomous: humans still choose targets, set objectives and oversee operations, while more intervening work can be delegated to machines. It reports that Storm-0940 used an agentic framework and that, in some cases, activity moved from target selection to privileged cloud access in less than three hours. The bulletin also recommends deciding in advance who may isolate accounts, applications, networks or services, and practising for incidents that unfold within hours rather than days. This is not a telecom-specific incident report. Source [14]: https://www.cyber.gc.ca/en/guidance/cyber-threat-bulletin-agentic-artificial-intelligence-accelerating-cyber-attacks.

2. **CERT-In Advisory CIAD-2026-0049 (15 October 2026).** Primary national cyber-advisory. Direct HTTP retrieval (200) and text extraction succeeded in this cycle. It reports that agentic tooling in a financially motivated campaign against Indian entities compressed the time from initial access to impact from days to a few hours; it also reports parallel activity and repeat attacks within days when fixes were incomplete. A third exact quote on repeat attacks was added to global citation-ledger [11]. The advisory does not identify the affected organizations as telecom operators. The chapter therefore attributes the observations to CERT-In and does not present them as telco incidents. Source [11]: https://cert-in.org.in/s2cMainServlet?VLCODE=CIAD-2026-0049&pageid=PUBVLNOTES02.

3. **Republic of Korea Ministry of Science and ICT (MSIT), “MSIT Releases Final Investigation Results on SK Telecom Data Breach” (published 10 July 2025).** Primary government investigation. MSIT reports that SK Telecom detected abnormally large outbound traffic at 11:20 p.m. on 18 April 2025 and notified KISA at 4:46 p.m. on 20 April, beyond the statutory 24-hour reporting window. A public-private investigation team was established on 23 April and conducted forensic inspections of all 42,605 SKT servers through 27 June; the final investigation identified 28 infected servers. The time pair documents detection and reporting, not time to containment or the start of the intrusion. This was a data breach, not evidence of a network outage, and it was not attributed to AI. Source [15]: https://www.msit.go.kr/eng/bbs/view.do?bbsSeqNo=42&nttSeqNo=1139.

4. **MSIT, “MSIT Releases First Investigation Results on SKT Data Breach” (29 April 2025).** Primary preliminary government update. The Ministry encouraged customers to replace their USIM cards or use a protection service and urged SKT to expand service channels and add reservations so more customers could receive protection promptly. This is used to show the customer-facing and operational work that accompanies technical response, not to claim a service outage. Source [16]: https://www.msit.go.kr/eng/bbs/view.do?bbsSeqNo=42&mId=4&mPid=2&nttSeqNo=1111&pageIndex=&sCode=eng&searchOpt=ALL&searchTxt=.
5. **CERT-In Advisory CIAD-2026-0058, “Escalation of AI-Driven Attacks on BFSI Entities by Threat Actor ‘CRAFTY FALCON’” (original issue date 4 November 2026).** Primary CERT-In/CSIRT-Fin advisory update concerning Indian banking, financial services and insurance entities. Direct HTTP retrieval (200) and text extraction succeeded in this cycle. It reports re-targeting after incomplete remediation and attacks timed to holidays, weekends and other low-monitoring windows. The chapter uses the off-hours point to frame a response-planning question; the campaign is not a telecom incident. The chapter-local [20] maps to global citation-ledger [35], with exact evidence quotes attached. Source [20]: https://cert-in.org.in/s2cMainServlet?VLCODE=CIAD-2026-0058&pageid=PUBVLNOTES02.

6. **CISA, FBI and international partners, “Communicating Under Pressure: Best Practices for Service Providers” (published 2 September 2026).** Official guidance for service providers and critical-infrastructure operators. The registered publication page [22] links to the official PDF, which was directly retrieved (HTTP 200) and text-extracted in this cycle. The PDF recommends outage-communications plans with triggers, escalation paths and customer, partner and regulatory templates; it calls for frequent updates on what is known, unknown and under investigation, while protecting operational security and aligning with law-enforcement and containment efforts. Applying those practices to defensive isolation that changes service availability is the chapter’s recommendation, not a claim made by the source. Four exact evidence quotes from the linked PDF are attached to global ledger [22]. The chapter continues to cite the landing page so its local [22] remains consistent with Chapter 4. Source [22]: https://www.cisa.gov/resources-tools/resources/communicating-under-pressure-best-practices-service-providers; linked PDF [36]: https://www.cisa.gov/sites/default/files/2026-09/joint-guidance-communicating-under-pressure-508c.pdf.

7. **Australian Cyber Security Centre, “Securing AI testing environments” (published and reviewed 12 November 2026).** Official guidance for organisations testing AI models and agents for cyber-security capability assessment or integration into cyber-defence workflows. The official page’s indexed excerpt describes real-time monitoring, logs stored beyond agent access, human oversight and stopping or containing tests at risk thresholds. Direct page/PDF retrieval timed out in the earlier cycle; in cycle 25, the PDF timed out again, the browser returned `ERR_HTTP2_PROTOCOL_ERROR`, and the recovery script found no archive copy. Source [24]: https://www.cyber.gov.au/publication/securing-ai-testing-environments; linked PDF [37]: https://www.cyber.gov.au/sites/default/files/2026-11/Securing%20AI%20testing%20environments.pdf.

8. **CERT-In Advisory CIAD-2026-0020, “Defending Against Frontier AI Driven Cyber Risks” (26 April 2026).** Primary Indian national cyber advisory. It recommends incident plans for accelerated exploitation, including emergency patching, containment and coordinated response; it also recommends tabletop exercises for five simultaneous incidents explicitly modelling AI-driven scenarios. This is cross-sector guidance, not evidence of a telco incident or a universal operator threshold. The official page was retrieved directly (HTTP 200); its full text was saved for verification. The chapter uses only the exercise recommendation. Source [28]: https://www.cert-in.org.in/s2cMainServlet?VLCODE=CIAD-2026-0020&pageid=PUBVLNOTES02.

9. **CISA, “Logging Reference Architecture” (publication month August 2026).** The PDF was directly retrieved and its text extracted. Its front matter identifies the intended audience as Federal Civilian Executive Branch agencies; the official publication page’s indexed excerpt gives 20 August 2026, but the chapter uses only the PDF’s verified August publication month and scope. The architecture says AI-assisted techniques can support incident triage, scoping, timeline reconstruction and summarization, while actions with material operational, legal or privacy consequences should remain subject to human review and established incident criteria. This is federal logging guidance, not a telecom requirement or evidence of telco practice. Three verbatim quotes are attached to global ledger [34]. The chapter cites the PDF directly as local [34]. Source [34]: https://www.cisa.gov/sites/default/files/2026-09/logging-reference-architecture-508.pdf; companion publication page [33]: https://www.cisa.gov/resources-tools/resources/logging-reference-architecture.

## Draft decisions and boundaries

- Reframed response speed as an operating-model issue, not merely SOC alerting. The chapter separates detection, decision, safe action, continuity and recovery clocks.
- Used the Canadian bulletin and CERT-In for recent evidence of compressed attack stages, with explicit attribution and caveats that neither source describes a telecom-specific attack.
- Used the SK Telecom case to ground the difference between detection, statutory reporting, forensic scoping and customer protection. It is not framed as AI-enabled or as a network-availability incident. The report’s reporting delay is not described as the full containment delay.
- Added a clearly labeled composite NOC/SOC scenario involving a suspicious network-management identity during an approved maintenance window. It is a constructed example, not a reported event. It illustrates how SOC evidence, NOC service knowledge, IAM actions, supplier input and decision authority must work together.
- The action model favors bounded, pre-authorized, reversible containment; service-impacting actions retain a named human decision owner and a tested fallback. The exact controls must be adapted to the operator’s architecture.
- The chapter ends by moving from the problem of time and authority to Chapter 4’s four practical moves. Chapter 4 was not drafted.
