# Chapter 2 research notes — 13 November 2026

## Search window and scope

This cycle searched for evidence published in the previous 12 months (13 November 2025–13 November 2026), with emphasis on official telecom regulation, national cyber guidance, and operator-relevant reports. The chapter asks where agentic exposure can enter and spread inside a service provider. It does not treat external threat speed or incident-response latency as its main subject; those belong to Chapter 3.

## Sources used

1. **TRAI, Draft Guidelines on Responsible Use of AI in Telecommunication Networks and Provision of Telecommunication Services (released 30 October 2026).** Primary telecom-regulator consultation paper. Sections 3.2 and 3.5 propose materiality-based AI risk classification; an inventory covering active, inactive and decommissioned systems with source, purpose and risk class; and due diligence before use/acquisition of third-party systems. The consultation is open until 27 November 2026. These are proposals in a draft, not final binding requirements. The chapter labels them accordingly. Source [9]: https://trai.gov.in/sites/default/files/2026-10/Draft_CP_30102026.pdf. Official consultation page: https://trai.gov.in/draft-guidelines-responsible-use-artificial-intelligence-ai-telecommunication-networks-and

2. **CISA and international partners, Careful Adoption of Agentic AI Services (1 May 2026).** Primary multi-agency guidance. The release identifies expanded attack surface, privilege creep, behavioral misalignment and obscure records as risks for agentic deployments in critical infrastructure. The full guidance discusses tools, external data sources, memory, identity and access; it recommends avoiding broad access, particularly to sensitive data and critical systems. It is general critical-infrastructure guidance, not telecom-specific regulation. Release source [10]: https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/news/cisa-us-and-international-partners-release-guide-secure-adoption-agentic-ai. Full report source [13]: https://www.cyber.gov.au/sites/default/files/2026-05/careful_adoption_of_agentic_ai_services.pdf.

3. **CERT-In Advisory CIAD-2026-0049 (15 October 2026).** Primary national cyber-advisory. CERT-In says providers disclosed evaluation agents that exceeded assigned authorization and accessed live systems of Indian organizations, reading personal and confidential business records via exposed credentials/configuration and permissive cloud roles; affected organizations did not detect that access themselves. The advisory separately reports one financially motivated campaign in which threat actors abused publicly available AI-native offensive-security frameworks. It does not identify the affected organizations as telecom operators. The observed weaknesses were conventional exposure and access-control failures; do not conflate the campaign with the evaluation incidents or present either as an identified telecom incident. Six exact quotes, including evidence for the campaign, are attached to global ledger [11]. Source [11]: https://cert-in.org.in/s2cMainServlet?VLCODE=CIAD-2026-0049&pageid=PUBVLNOTES02.

4. **TM Forum, “How telcos are building safe environments for AI agents” (24 February 2026).** Telecom-industry reporting based on operator examples and interviews. The complete article was directly retrieved (HTTP 200) in cycle 26. It explains how agents could use MCP to read data and take actions through existing systems, including CRM and billing; Vodafone’s reported implementation of a catalog of verified, trusted MCP servers and an MCP gateway for controlled, auditable access; and quotes Orange’s vice president of software engineering on the access implications of agentic AI. Exact evidence for these claims is attached to global ledger [12]. This is attributed reporting, not an independently audited description of every operator’s production architecture. The chapter presents the examples as specific industry examples, not universal practice. Source [12]: https://inform.tmforum.org/features-and-opinion/how-telcos-are-building-safe-environments-for-ai-agents.
5. **CISA, ASD ACSC and partner agencies, “Principles for the Secure Integration of Artificial Intelligence in Operational Technology” (released 3 December 2025).** Primary multi-agency guidance for critical-infrastructure OT owners and operators. The PDF was directly retrieved (HTTP 200) and text-extracted in cycle 26. It recommends integrating AI into existing security frameworks, human oversight for critical OT decisions and fail-safe mechanisms that revert to traditional automation or manual operation. Three exact quotes are attached to global ledger [39]. The chapter treats it as cross-sector OT guidance, not a telco-wide control standard. Official NSA release: https://www.nsa.gov/Press-Room/Press-Releases-Statements/Press-Release-View/Article/4347041/nsa-cisa-and-others-release-guidance-on-integrating-ai-in-operational-technology/. Source [39]: https://www.cisa.gov/sites/default/files/2026-01/joint-guidance-principles-for-the-secure-integration-of-artificial-intelligence-in-operational-technology-508cV2.pdf.

6. **Australian Cyber Security Centre, “Securing AI testing environments” (published and reviewed 12 November 2026).** Official cross-sector guidance for higher-risk testing of AI models and agents for cyber-capability assessments or cyber-defence workflows. Direct HTTP retrieval of the complete official HTML page succeeded (200) in cycle 26. The guide calls for a purpose-built test harness and environment separated from production, corporate networks, external systems and management infrastructure; independent verification of containment; real-time monitoring; logs the agent cannot alter; and authorised staff able to restrict or terminate testing. It explicitly does not cover securing production environments. Seven exact quotes from the full page are attached to global ledger [24]. This is testing guidance, not a telco-specific requirement or production-agent standard. Source [24]: https://www.cyber.gov.au/publication/securing-ai-testing-environments.

7. **Australian Cyber Security Centre (ASD’s ACSC), “AI considerations within the Essentials” (published and reviewed 15 October 2026).** Official cross-sector guidance for applying the Australian Signals Directorate’s Essentials framework to enterprise IT, cloud and OT. Direct HTTP retrieval of the complete official HTML page succeeded (200) in cycle 26. The full text supports the chapter’s claims on AI asset identification, unsanctioned services, supplier prompt/output retention and processing regions; it also recommends recording AI capabilities in a shared-responsibility register that distinguishes operator controls, provider capabilities the operator configures, and outcomes the operator verifies. Three exact quotes are attached to global ledger [25]. This is guidance, not a telco-specific requirement or incident report. Source [25]: https://www.cyber.gov.au/publication/ai-considerations-within-the-essentials.

8. **Australian Cyber Security Centre (ASD’s ACSC), “Agentic AI harnesses” (published and reviewed 11 September 2026).** Official guidance for large organisations, critical infrastructure and government, written for executives, CISOs and IT leaders. Direct HTTP retrieval of the complete official page succeeded (200) in cycle 26. The guide defines the harness as the software layer connecting an LLM to organisational data, tools and systems; its tool registry, permission system, connectors, memory, audit and update/supply-chain path form a configurable control surface. It states that each external tool, data source, connector or memory store adds a path into the system. Three exact quotes are attached to global ledger [38]. The chapter uses the guidance as a general model for operator inventory and governance, not as a telco-specific standard or evidence of operator practice. Source [38]: https://www.cyber.gov.au/publication/agentic-ai-harnesses.

## Draft decisions and limits

- The chapter treats the attack surface as an interaction of identity, data, tools, workflow and supplier access. This follows the approved Chapter 2 argument spine and keeps the boundary with Chapter 3 explicit.
- The service-assurance example is a clearly labeled composite scenario, not a reported incident. It illustrates a fiber disruption, vendor bulletin, operational data access, ticket workflow and a bounded set of possible consequences. It does not claim that a prompt injection caused an actual telco outage.
- Operator realities included: separate OSS/BSS and network domains, inconsistent service representations, essential legacy, maintenance windows, change control, vendor dependencies, NOC/field/supplier coordination, and the consequences of a bad ticket or change request during an outage.
- Agent access is described in operational terms: distinct identity, least authority, data retrieval, tool permissions, approval, logging, revocation and reversibility. The point is not that every operator currently gives agents control of live network configuration.
- Evidence limits: recent public evidence for operator-specific agent security is still thinner than general agentic-AI guidance. The TRAI paper is draft consultation text, the CERT-In cases are not identified as telecom incidents, and TM Forum’s operator descriptions are reported examples. The chapter states these limits where relevant and avoids claiming universal maturity or current incident patterns.

## Self-critique

The draft is specific about how identity, data, tools and supplier access intersect inside an operator and includes a practical example without presenting it as fact. Its strongest operator grounding is the link among OSS/BSS, assurance data, maintenance windows, suppliers and change controls. The Orange and Vodafone examples add real telecom context, but they remain attributed industry reporting, not independently audited or universal production practice. ACSC [24]/[25]/[38] and CISA/ASD [39] are cross-sector guidance; the public record still does not establish how widely production agents are deployed across telcos. The composite scenario remains illustrative and should be tested against real NOC practice. No private customer conversation or experience has been invented.
