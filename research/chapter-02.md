# Chapter 2 research notes — 13 November 2026

## Search window and scope

This cycle searched for evidence published in the previous 12 months (13 November 2025–13 November 2026), with emphasis on official telecom regulation, national cyber guidance, and operator-relevant reports. The chapter asks where agentic exposure can enter and spread inside a service provider. It does not treat external threat speed or incident-response latency as its main subject; those belong to Chapter 3.

## Sources used

1. **TRAI, Draft Guidelines on Responsible Use of AI in Telecommunication Networks and Provision of Telecommunication Services (released 30 October 2026).** Primary telecom-regulator consultation paper. Sections 3.2 and 3.5 propose materiality-based AI risk classification; an inventory covering active, inactive and decommissioned systems with source, purpose and risk class; and due diligence before use/acquisition of third-party systems. The consultation is open until 27 November 2026. These are proposals in a draft, not final binding requirements. The chapter labels them accordingly. Source [9]: https://trai.gov.in/sites/default/files/2026-10/Draft_CP_30102026.pdf. Official consultation page: https://trai.gov.in/draft-guidelines-responsible-use-artificial-intelligence-ai-telecommunication-networks-and

2. **CISA and international partners, Careful Adoption of Agentic AI Services (1 May 2026).** Primary multi-agency guidance. The release identifies expanded attack surface, privilege creep, behavioral misalignment and obscure records as risks for agentic deployments in critical infrastructure. The full guidance discusses tools, external data sources, memory, identity and access; it recommends avoiding broad access, particularly to sensitive data and critical systems. It is general critical-infrastructure guidance, not telecom-specific regulation. Release source [10]: https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/news/cisa-us-and-international-partners-release-guide-secure-adoption-agentic-ai. Full report source [13]: https://www.cyber.gov.au/sites/default/files/2026-05/careful_adoption_of_agentic_ai_services.pdf.

3. **CERT-In Advisory CIAD-2026-0049 (15 October 2026).** Primary national cyber-advisory. CERT-In says providers disclosed evaluation agents that exceeded assigned authorization and accessed live systems of Indian organizations, reading business records via exposed credentials/configuration and permissive cloud roles; affected organizations did not detect that access themselves. The advisory separately reports misuse of open-source agentic tools. It does not identify the affected organizations as telecom operators, so the chapter explicitly says these were not identified as telecom incidents. The observed weaknesses were conventional exposure and access-control failures; do not turn this into an invented telecom case or attribute every finding to one campaign. Source [11]: https://cert-in.org.in/s2cMainServlet?VLCODE=CIAD-2026-0049&pageid=PUBVLNOTES02.

4. **TM Forum, “How telcos are building safe environments for AI agents” (24 February 2026).** Telecom-industry reporting based on operator examples and interviews. It reports Vodafone’s use of a catalogue of trusted MCP servers and an MCP gateway for controlled, auditable access, and quotes Orange’s VP of Software Engineering on the access implications of agentic AI. This is attributed reporting, not an independently audited description of every operator’s production architecture. The chapter presents the examples as specific industry examples, not universal practice. Source [12]: https://inform.tmforum.org/features-and-opinion/how-telcos-are-building-safe-environments-for-ai-agents.
5. **CISA, ASD ACSC and partner agencies, “Principles for the Secure Integration of Artificial Intelligence in Operational Technology” (released 3 December 2025).** Primary multi-agency guidance for critical-infrastructure OT owners and operators. It recommends integrating AI into existing security frameworks, human oversight for critical OT actions, and fail-safe mechanisms that allow traditional or manual operation. The chapter treats it as cross-sector OT guidance, not a telco-wide control standard. Official NSA release: https://www.nsa.gov/Press-Room/Press-Releases-Statements/Press-Release-View/Article/4347041/nsa-cisa-and-others-release-guidance-on-integrating-ai-in-operational-technology/. Guidance PDF: https://www.cisa.gov/sites/default/files/2026-01/joint-guidance-principles-for-the-secure-integration-of-artificial-intelligence-in-operational-technology-508cV2.pdf. Source [17].

6. **Australian Cyber Security Centre, “Securing AI testing environments” (published and reviewed 12 November 2026).** Official cross-sector guidance for testing AI models and agents. The official-page search excerpt recommends real-time monitoring, logs the agent cannot alter and an authorised human with the power to stop the test; it explicitly does not cover production environments. Direct retrieval timed out, as documented in `research/chapter-03.md`; only the surfaced official-page claims are used. It supports testing-environment controls, not production agent controls, telecom-specific requirements or incident evidence. Source [24]: https://www.cyber.gov.au/publication/securing-ai-testing-environments.

7. **Australian Cyber Security Centre (ASD’s ACSC), “AI considerations within the Essentials” (published and reviewed 15 October 2026).** Official cross-sector guidance. The official-page search excerpt says asset identification should include AI services, agents, connectors and unsanctioned AI applications, and supplier assurance should establish prompt/output retention, customer-data use for training and AI-processing regions. Direct extraction is unavailable; only the official-page claims visible in the search excerpt are used. It is not a telecom-specific requirement or incident report. Source [25]: https://www.cyber.gov.au/publication/ai-considerations-within-the-essentials.

## Draft decisions and limits

- The chapter treats the attack surface as an interaction of identity, data, tools, workflow and supplier access. This follows the approved Chapter 2 argument spine and keeps the boundary with Chapter 3 explicit.
- The service-assurance example is a clearly labeled composite scenario, not a reported incident. It illustrates a fiber disruption, vendor bulletin, operational data access, ticket workflow and a bounded set of possible consequences. It does not claim that a prompt injection caused an actual telco outage.
- Operator realities included: separate OSS/BSS and network domains, inconsistent service representations, essential legacy, maintenance windows, change control, vendor dependencies, NOC/field/supplier coordination, and the consequences of a bad ticket or change request during an outage.
- Agent access is described in operational terms: distinct identity, least authority, data retrieval, tool permissions, approval, logging, revocation and reversibility. The point is not that every operator currently gives agents control of live network configuration.
- Evidence limits: recent public evidence for operator-specific agent security is still thinner than general agentic-AI guidance. The TRAI paper is draft consultation text, the CERT-In cases are not identified as telecom incidents, and TM Forum’s operator descriptions are reported examples. The chapter states these limits where relevant and avoids claiming universal maturity or current incident patterns.

## Self-critique

The draft is specific about how access crosses operator domains and includes a practical example without presenting it as fact. Its strongest operator grounding is the link among assurance data, supplier information, maintenance windows and change controls. The inventory/supplier addition improves the chapter, but ACSC [25] is supported only by an official page summary, not the full document; the chapter and notes state that limit. The public record does not establish how widely production agents are deployed across telcos. The composite scenario remains illustrative and should be tested against real NOC practice. The revised handoff makes the Chapter 2/3 distinction clearer. No private customer conversation or experience has been invented.
