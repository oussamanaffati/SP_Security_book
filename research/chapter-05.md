# Chapter 5 research notes — 13 November 2026

## Scope and source window

Chapter 5 turns the four moves into a board decision: service consequence, evidence, proposed action, resource choice and residual risk. Recent source work focused on board governance and critical-infrastructure investment and AI-service-resilience guidance, with a current telecom-regulatory signal. The CISA publications are within the rolling 12-month window; TRAI’s consultation is current. The UK Cyber Governance Code was published on 8 April 2025, outside the rolling 12-month window. It is used as an extant government governance reference, not described as a new development or universal requirement.

## Sources used

1. **UK Department for Science, Innovation and Technology / NCSC, Cyber Governance Code of Practice (published 8 April 2025).** Official UK government board guidance. It says cyber incidents can disrupt business continuity and damage customer trust. Its actions cover critical services, senior risk ownership, risk appetite, supplier risk, resource allocation, annual exercises, quarterly reporting with metrics and tolerances, and awareness of relevant regulatory obligations. The chapter describes it as UK guidance, not binding law for every operator. Source [18]: https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/cyber-governance-code-of-practice/cyber-governance-code-of-practice.

2. **CISA, Cybersecurity Performance Goals 2.0 for Critical Infrastructure (released 11 December 2025).** Official U.S. critical-infrastructure guidance. CISA describes the goals as measurable, outcome-oriented protections for IT and OT, with a governance component focused on accountability and risk management; it presents them as a baseline for investment and measurable progress. The chapter uses this to support outcome-based assurance, not as a mandatory framework for all service providers. Source [19]: https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/alerts/2025/12/11/cybersecurity-performance-goals-20-critical-infrastructure.

3. **TRAI, Draft Guidelines on Responsible Use of AI in Telecommunication Networks (published for consultation 30 October 2026).** Current telecom-regulator proposal. The draft remains a consultation, not a final rule. The chapter uses it to illustrate why board papers should distinguish binding requirements from proposed guidance and identify applicability by market. Sections 3.1 and 3.6 propose board approval of a covered entity’s internal AI policy and of an AI Governance and Safety Committee overseeing implementation. The committee is proposed to include relevant organizational functions, including network operations, service delivery, IT, data management, regulatory and consumer affairs. These are proposals, not current requirements outside the draft’s scope or before adoption. Source [9]: https://trai.gov.in/sites/default/files/2026-10/Draft_CP_30102026.pdf.

4. **CISA, Canadian Centre for Cyber Security and partners, “Managing AI Token Consumption for Operational Resilience” (published 22 October 2026).** Official cross-sector critical-infrastructure guidance. It says token-based service models can affect cost, performance and access to critical services; unmanaged consumption can contribute to unpredictable cost, reduced availability, degraded performance, token exhaustion and denial-of-tokens/denial-of-wallet attacks. It advises critical-infrastructure owners to identify mission-critical functions that depend on these services and plan for reduced availability during outages, incidents or high demand. Chapter 5 uses it to add a board-evidence question about metered AI-service dependencies, their performance/availability consequences and fallback; it is not evidence of a telecom incident. Source [21]: https://www.cisa.gov/resources-tools/resources/managing-ai-token-consumption-operational-resilience.

5. **CISA, “Five Eyes Cyber Security Agencies Statement” (22 June 2026).** Cross-sector statement by the leaders of the Five Eyes cyber security agencies on AI and cyber risk. It says cyber risk is a core business risk and leadership responsibility, and that boards and executives should ensure cyber resilience works under pressure; controls must perform during an incident, not just exist. The chapter uses it as board-assurance guidance, not as a telecom-specific rule or evidence of a telco incident. Source [23]: https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/news/five-eyes-cyber-security-agencies-statement.

## Considered but not used for article-level legal claims

## Draft decisions and boundaries

- The board conversation follows a repeatable sequence: service at stake → system/workflow → evidence and uncertainty → action → residual risk → explicit decision.
- Reframed the four moves as board decisions about priorities, accountable owners, protected capacity and quarterly assurance, rather than a technology purchase request.
- Made board evidence concrete: consequential AI-enabled workflows, access boundaries, exercise findings, service fallback, regulatory obligations and roadmap dependencies.
- Included one clearly labeled composite board dialogue. It is constructed; no customer, board member or private operator conversation is represented as real.
- Added explicit language to avoid: unsupported claims of an AI-caused national outage, compliance-as-proof-of-safety, zero-risk promises, tool-first pitches and unsubstantiated financial loss estimates.
- Ended with a non-hype 12-month call to action. The chapter is the final chapter; no further chapter transition is added.

## Self-critique

The chapter gives a CISO/CTO specific board asks and a defensible evidence path. It avoids claiming that AI guarantees an outage and treats residual risk as a choice to own. The composite dialogue is short and bounded, but it may still sound scripted; Oussama should adapt it to his natural boardroom voice. The public governance guidance is cross-sector rather than telco-specific, and no private board exchange or real operator case was available for this chapter. The prose is direct, avoids hype and em dashes, and closes the book with action rather than prediction.
