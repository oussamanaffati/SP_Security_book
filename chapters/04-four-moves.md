# Chapter 4: Four Moves for Service Providers in the Next 12 Months

The same identity may connect an OSS query, a service ticket and an operational action. That shortens the path between information and change, but it can also let a mistake or compromised tool travel farther before a person intervenes. The Canadian Centre for Cyber Security’s cross-sector evidence suggests AI can shorten parts of an attacker’s workflow. For a service provider, the task is to contain both risks without putting continuity at odds with security.

Existing security, network, architecture, supplier and change processes provide a natural starting point. The review is about where agent identities, tools or supplier features fall outside them: which identities can reach which data or tools, which actions need approval, how NOC and SOC teams will rehearse a response, and what remains uncovered.

The sequence is useful because each move answers a different question. Visibility shows where authority exists. Access review tests whether it matches the task. Rehearsal reveals whether the operator can contain failure and maintain service. The roadmap gives owners and resources to close gaps. The moves can overlap; a complete inventory is not a precondition for addressing a high-impact path already known.

## Move 1: Visibility means more than an AI inventory

Two questions frame the work: where can AI reach the operator’s systems or data, and what actions can it take? A joint review can bring together Network Operations, IT, security, architecture, customer care, procurement, privacy and key suppliers. Its scope can include pilots, SaaS features and automation, even when no one calls them AI.

A service-first review is more useful than a model-first list. The relevant areas include OSS/BSS, service assurance, network management, customer identity, field operations, cloud control planes and supplier access. A customer-care summary tool has a different service impact from an assistant that can reach network configuration or subscriber records.

A concise record can capture the owner and purpose, model or supplier, identity, data and tools, affected services, available logs, fallback, relevant markets and who can suspend the use. Record whether context is task-scoped, persistent or not used; for persistent memory, capture the read/write identity, the data owner for permitted content and the platform/supplier owner who can evidence scope and clearing. Link to Chapter 2’s memory check; a prompt/output-retention statement alone does not verify that boundary. Open questions should remain visible rather than being marked complete.

An inventory becomes useful when it links a capability to its service owner, data, identity and available actions. Release notices, procurement reviews and architecture changes can feed the same inventory, so the register remains useful after the initial discovery. When the supplier cannot explain the feature’s identity or reach, record the assurance gap and its owner rather than marking the entry complete.

The Australian Cyber Security Centre’s October 2026 guidance calls for AI asset identification to include services, agents, connectors and unsanctioned applications. It also recommends checking what providers retain in prompts and outputs, whether customer data is used to train models, and where processing occurs. This is cross-sector guidance; local legal duties still need review.

Chapter 2 discusses TRAI’s telecom consultation draft. It is not binding; Legal and Compliance should confirm which inventory and third-party due-diligence duties apply in each market.

Many operators can make the first pass manageable by focusing on uses that could affect a critical service or sensitive data. Project, procurement, cloud and supplier records can help surface them; workflow owners can clarify what remains uncertain.

**Use discovery sources as leads, not as proof of absence.** A project register may identify an approved pilot but miss an assistant introduced through a routine product update. Procurement records can show a supplier relationship but not which feature is enabled. IAM and target configuration can reveal a service identity and its permissions, but not whether the service owner considers those permissions necessary or what fallback a revocation would remove. Workflow-owner interviews explain purpose and operational dependency, but a person may not know that a connector or supplier changed. Runtime logs show activity that was observed during their retention period; they do not prove that a dormant route or unlogged action cannot occur. No single source justifies a complete or “not applicable” status.

Under limited capacity, start with the most consequential service paths and cross-check at least one source of intended use against one source of deployed or observed behavior. For the supplier diagnostic feature, a release notice might reveal that the capability exists; the network-management identity and target logs are needed to establish what it can reach and did. For the shared account, IAM permission data can expose edge/core reach, while service owners establish which workflows depend on it and what removal would affect. Put unresolved discrepancies in the record. A difference between the service description and deployed permission is a decision signal, not a clerical issue.

A useful discovery record distinguishes **confirmed**, **inferred** and **unknown**. “No feature found in the register” is not “no feature exists.” “No target event in the available logs” is not “no access path exists” unless the log scope, identity coverage and period support that conclusion. Assign an evidence owner and next check to each material unknown. This lets the CISO act on a known high-impact path now while the broader inventory remains incomplete, and prevents an unfinished search from being presented as assurance.

Use four evidence levels, and state which claim each supports. Level 1, supplier assertion or design documentation, describes intended behavior; it does not prove deployed permissions. Level 2, deployed-configuration review, shows configured identities, data and tools, but may miss runtime or downstream behavior. Level 3, a bounded technical test, shows what happened in the tested path, including allowed and denied requests, revocation and logs; it does not prove untested paths or live-service safety. Level 4, operational exercise or observation, tests who decides, what fallback works and how service is affected; a tabletop cannot prove technical enforcement. More than one level may be needed. These are evidence classes, not a score; report scope, limitations and the next evidence needed to support the decision.

For workflows that join operational records, use Chapter 2’s source-contract check to test whether the output preserves provenance and surfaces conflicts. It supports that data-quality decision, not a claim that the agent is generally accurate or secure.

**Treat retrieved instructions as data, not authority.** The NOC assistant can open supplier cases and reach neighboring-domain information, but the composite does not establish what external case text it retrieves or how it responds to instruction-like content. **Educational test assumption:** a supplier case or alarm annotation contains a request to suppress a visibility warning or fetch data outside the stated task. Test with synthetic or safely replayed content in a non-production path. The question is not whether a prompt filter recognizes every phrase. It is whether the assistant stays within the approved task, exposes the source to its reviewer, and whether the connector and target refuse any out-of-scope read or action regardless of what the content asks.

If the text changes the recommendation but the assistant has only bounded read/draft authority, the operator still needs to see the source and decide whether to use the proposal; the output is not proof of a safe condition. If the content leads to an out-of-scope tool call that the target accepts, the tested boundary has failed and the affected action path should remain disabled or restricted pending correction and retest. If the target denies the request but the event is not attributable, the control may stop the action while leaving an investigation gap. Record both outcomes. Do not run this test against live network controls or real customer data unless the operator has an approved test design and service fallback. Review whether the added source filtering, review and test effort is justified by the diagnostic value; if not, remove that retrieved-content path rather than widening authority.

Ownership is often split. The platform team may administer the feature, Procurement may hold the supplier relationship, and a service executive may carry the impact if it fails. A single “owner” field can hide those differences. Record separately who can suspend the feature, who supplies control evidence and which service may be affected. Chapter 5 defines the acceptance record; the register should link to it rather than repeat its authority test.

**Put supplier changes through a risk-triggered release gate.** The diagnostic feature is a useful test of that gate: a routine update can add capability without changing the product name. Keep a baseline of identity, data, connectors, action rights, provider, processing and retention, supplier access, logs and disablement. Compare each release with the last tested boundary. Reassess material changes; leave verified unchanged properties in the normal change process. Seek advance notice and evidence commitments where commercially feasible, but compare supplier claims with deployed configuration. Missing notice is an assurance gap, not proof that nothing changed.

A new source, identity, connector, action right, processing or retention change, supplier access path, or loss of independent disablement should trigger a targeted review. Retest the changed path rather than repeating unrelated checks, and set review frequency from the consequence of undetected drift and how well the operator can observe it. If the deployed state cannot be compared with the tested baseline, treat the earlier result as stale. Keep the feature off or within the last demonstrably enforced boundary, if that restriction itself is verified. Supplier coordination, engineering tests and NOC backfill are recurring work; estimate them from local release volume and change practice.

Keep maintenance moving. If a release also carries a fix that local evidence says should not wait, establish whether the deployed version is affected and reachable, then ask for the supplier’s supported separation, backport, mitigation, test and rollback options. The service owner confirms impact and fallback if diagnostics or monitoring change. Where the capability cannot be separated, compare the supported combined change with delaying the fix; do not assume a workaround exists or treat a booked window as risk reduction. After deployment, verify the fix, feature state, target permissions, attributable logs, NOC visibility and rollback. Stop further rollout if the actual path or service view differs from the approved boundary.

**Worked decision: supplier diagnostic feature.** The composite establishes that a routine update enabled an AI diagnostic feature that reads topology and proposes actions, and that the feature was missing from the operator’s AI project register. Direct execution is not established.

**Educational assumptions for this decision:** Operations Engineering owns the feature; the only evidence in hand is Level 1 supplier design material; a separate feature switch is claimed but untested; manual diagnosis is possible during a supported window if the NOC receives planned backfill. These assumptions make the decision complete for learning; replace them with operator evidence before use.

| Evidence | What it supports | What it does not establish |
|---|---|---|
| Composite facts | Feature presence, topology reads and proposed actions. | Deployed identity, downstream write path, data destination or operational impact. |
| Supplier design material | Intended behavior, under the assumptions above. | Deployed permissions, supplier-side processing/retention, target enforcement or stop behavior. |
| Evidence needed for a release decision | Level 2 configuration review; supplier data-path and support-access details; Level 3 allowed-read and denied-action test with attributable logs and revocation; service-owner confirmation of monitoring, fallback and manual workload. | The test and review have not been completed in this educational decision. |

| Option | Benefit and cost | Gate |
|---|---|---|
| Enable as shipped | Keeps the diagnostic capability available and avoids immediate manual work, but leaves authority and service effects unverified. | Not supported by Level 1 evidence alone. |
| Keep the product release; hold the feature off | Preserves other product functions if the feature can be isolated; requires a runtime check and supported rollback. | Use only when the switch blocks runtime calls and downstream actions, and essential NOC visibility remains. |
| Pause or roll back the combined release; seek a supported separation or mitigation | Avoids introducing an unbounded feature, but may delay diagnostics or an unrelated fix and return work to the NOC. | Confirm supplier support, service impact, backfill and a safe window; do not assume a feature-disabled build or workaround exists. |

Under these assumptions, retain the product release only if a runtime test proves the feature can be held off independently and the rollback is supported. Leave the feature disabled while completing the configuration review and target test. If separation fails, use a supported pause or rollback after the service owner confirms the operational effect and fallback. If the package also carries a fix that may need to proceed sooner, verify that it applies to a reachable local version and obtain the supplier’s supported alternatives before comparing the combined change with delay. An urgent label alone does not settle service readiness.

The recommendation changes if deployed evidence shows a wider data or action path, the switch does not stop downstream access, or the fallback cannot preserve the required service view; those findings strengthen the case to pause or restrict the feature. If configuration and target tests show a bounded read/draft path, attributable logging and effective revocation, while the service owner confirms acceptable fallback and review capacity, management may consider a limited pilot for the tested task. That would not establish safety for broader data, users or actions.

Close the release-handling decision only when its record names the product version and affected systems, selected route, verified identity/data/action boundary, supplier processing and support evidence, target allow/deny results, principal and revocation logs, NOC visibility, manual fallback, rollback, owners and review trigger. Any unresolved element remains open or becomes an explicitly time-bounded decision by an executive with authority over the affected service. Enablement beyond the tested scope is a separate decision. Reopen this one if a supplier or product change alters the boundary or the fallback stops working.

Inventory shows where to look. Access review shows whether an agent has more authority than its task requires.

## Move 2: Match authority to the task

The review can start with identities and tools that have the greatest potential service impact. An assistant that can read subscriber records, open a change request or reach a network-management API deserves closer scrutiny than one limited to public guidance.

The core questions are what data the agent can read, what actions it can take, and whether its access can be stopped without disabling a surrounding service.

Where the platform allows it, the agent can use a dedicated identity rather than an employee account or broad shared-service permissions. Access can be limited to the data and tools needed for the task. Short-lived credentials issued only when needed may help. Logs should show which identity acted, what tool it called and what happened.

A configuration describes the intended boundary; it does not establish what the deployed integration can reach. For one or two high-impact uses, trace an allowed and an out-of-scope request from the agent identity through the connector to the target system. Confirm that the excluded access is refused, logs identify the action and principal, and revocation closes the relevant path. Include any persistent memory as a separate read/write path and apply Chapter 2’s synthetic-marker check for scope and clearing; supplier documentation alone is not an operator test. Test in a suitable environment or supported window without disrupting monitoring or service assurance. If a supplier-managed control cannot be verified directly, record that as an assurance limitation rather than treating the design description as proof.

It helps to distinguish read, recommend and execute. Read-only access is often a sensible start. A low-impact, reversible action may fit within clear limits and monitoring. Actions that could change production, affect customers or interrupt an essential workflow should remain within the operator’s normal authorization and change process. An agent can prepare a change; an authorized person approves and validates it.

A human approval step is effective only if the reviewer can see the evidence behind the recommendation, understand the affected service and decline the action. If the reviewer sees only a generated summary or a pre-filled change request, the approval may become procedural. Control effectiveness depends on whether the person has enough context and authority to make a different decision, not on how many approval boxes appear in the workflow.

**Decide when a recommendation may become a pre-authorized action.** In the NOC-assistant composite, the assistant proposed a visibility-reducing change; it was not executed. Under current facts, keep it as a draft in the operator’s normal change path. If repeated work later justifies pre-authorization, use a defined standard-change class with an exact task, target, parameter and service-condition envelope. Chapter 3 owns incident-time containment authority; keep that route separate from routine change approval. The assistant may assemble evidence but cannot approve or expand its own envelope.

A future envelope must be enforced at the target, not just described in a prompt or ticket. In a safe environment or supported window, test permitted and prohibited parameters, stale or conflicting inputs, duplicate or interrupted requests, attribution and rollback. The service owner should define recovery as a restored operational view: required feeds, alarms, histories and, where relevant, downstream ticket state, verified by the NOC. Reverting a configuration may not recover events that were never observed. Test suspension and revocation separately using the stop path below.

Draft review uses qualified NOC time; a narrow envelope needs platform engineering, target validation and service-owner review; general execution shifts effort into assurance and incident response, where a mistake can affect service before review. Measure local waiting, correction and recovery effort. If approval is not the bottleneck, wider authority will not solve it. Consider a defined standard action only after target enforcement and service recovery both meet local tolerances.

**Build the stop path outside the assistant.** A platform “pause” can prevent new model runs but may not revoke credentials already issued, end active sessions or cancel queued tool calls. Revoking the identity may stop new access but can also remove alarm reads and supplier-case handling if the workflow shares one principal. A target-side deny can block the prohibited action even when another route or connector remains active, but a broad deny may interrupt an essential workflow. These controls answer different questions; an on-screen inactive status does not prove the downstream path has stopped.

The NOC-assistant composite establishes a service account with neighboring-domain reach and the ability to open cases and propose a visibility-reducing change. It does not establish whether the assistant has a dedicated identity, whether queued actions exist, or whether target-side denial is available. **Educational acceptance assumption:** before a bounded pilot, the operator can use a separate service identity for alarm reads and a distinct action path, and the target can deny the action independently. Replace this with configuration and supplier evidence. Test that pausing the agent blocks new requests, revocation ends or bounds existing sessions, queued work cannot execute after stop, direct or alternate routes receive the intended target response, and essential alarms remain visible or move to a tested fallback. Capture the principal and result in the source-system logs.

Give an on-duty NOC or incident lead a usable stop route that does not depend on the assistant, the same identity or a supplier contact who may be unavailable. Define who may invoke it, what service impact the action can cause and who activates the fallback. If those paths cannot be tested safely, do not claim that containment is ready; keep the workflow at draft or restrict the target scope through a verified boundary. Chapter 3 covers the incident-time decision; this design work ensures there is something reliable to invoke.

**Do not treat reviewer capacity as unlimited.** For an educational planning exercise, assume a surge produces more NOC-assistant summaries and proposed updates than the on-duty team can inspect promptly. The existing composite establishes neither such a surge nor a reviewer backlog. Requiring review of every output preserves human control but can delay triage and turn review into rapid approval. Letting the assistant classify its own proposals as low risk can reduce queue length, but the system may miss a service dependency or treat a familiar action as safe in a different domain. A risk-tiered queue can reserve qualified review for outputs that alter service decisions, while keeping bounded read/draft work available; it needs clear rules enforced outside the model.

Set the local gate from service impact and demonstrated workload, not a universal queue-time target. Reviewers should see the source, data age, affected service, proposed action, target and rollback before approving. The agent cannot lower its own review tier or expand the permitted action during a surge. If a queue exceeds the service owner’s tolerance, pause the proposal/action path, keep an approved read-only view if it remains safe, and use the tested manual process. Test burst conditions with representative or synthetic inputs, recording queue age, corrections, missed context, reviewer availability, service view and fallback. Include NOC backfill, workflow engineering and supplier support in the cost. If no safe manual path or target-enforced boundary is verified, do not expand authority to compensate for the staffing gap.

CISA and its international partners advise limiting agent access to sensitive data and critical systems, and starting with low-risk uses. This is general guidance, not a telco control standard. Legacy platforms and supplier connections matter as much as modern APIs. If a legacy system cannot support a separate identity, a controlled gateway may help. Until then, read-only access may be safer.

**Illustrative read/recommend/execute authority matrix.** Adapt the boundaries to service impact and the operator’s architecture; the matrix is a decision aid, not a universal policy.

| Level | What the agent may do | Boundary and evidence | Decision consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Read** | Retrieve only task-required data. | Enforce read-only at the target or controlled connector. Test allowed/denied requests; confirm principal in logs; verify retention and destination. | Neighboring-domain data needs separate, time-bound authorization. Unverified source or identity means read-only is not low risk. |
| **Recommend** | Draft a diagnosis or action; do not submit changes. | Show source records and service context. Test that no hidden write path or downstream trigger exists. | Keeps the choice with the operator, but incomplete evidence can misdirect triage. |
| **Execute** | Perform only a narrow, deliberately delegated action. | Scope target/parameters; set limits; test stop, audit and rollback; preserve normal change control. | If unbounded, unobserved or unreversible, stay at recommendation. Delegated action needs an owner and service fallback. |

The level should follow the consequence, not the product label. A ticket update may be low impact in one workflow and operationally significant in another. Before moving a use from read to recommendation or execution, the service owner should see test evidence that the new boundary holds and understand what work becomes slower or manual if the agent is restricted. Security validates identity and control behavior; widening authority depends on the tested boundary and the service impact of restriction.

**When a task outlasts temporary access.** In an educational variant of Chapter 2’s NOC-assistant case, assume a time-bounded cross-domain read role has been approved for one incident, but the work is not complete when its expiry approaches. Setting a long expiry avoids a second approval but leaves broader access in place after it may be needed. Automatic renewal can turn a temporary exception into standing privilege without a new service decision. Requiring a fresh approval preserves review, but can delay triage if the authorized owner or deputy is unavailable. The composite does not establish an actual expiry, delay or test result.

Use an explicit renewal only when the service owner confirms the task still needs the additional fields and the authorized executive or named deputy approves the same bounded purpose, target and scope. Record what changed, why the original window was insufficient, which data was accessed, who approved the extension and when access must end. Do not let the agent or its generated output extend its own authority. A request to add write actions or another service domain is a new access decision, not a renewal of read access. If approval cannot arrive within the service tolerance, move to the tested manual path rather than silently lengthening the privilege.

Expiry counts only if the target blocks new calls and relevant existing sessions or queued actions are ended or bounded. Test those behaviors and the resulting logs before relying on the role. If the target cannot enforce them, the wall-clock expiry in IAM does not make access effectively time-bounded; keep the use human-mediated or restrict it to a verified boundary. Repeated renewals for ordinary work are evidence that the role or workflow needs redesign, not a reason to remove expiry. This process adds IAM, service-owner and NOC hand-off effort, but keeps each extension tied to a current service need and an accountable decision.

**Shared edge/core service account.** Chapter 1 establishes that one account reaches edge nodes and parts of the core. Moving to separate access may reduce reach and improve attribution, but the operator still has to identify dependent workflows and prove the new boundary after the change.

Separating that access can improve attribution and limit the path between edge and core, but the boundary also has to be operated. Account ownership, credential rotation, supplier support and rollback determine whether it remains effective. A gateway may help where a legacy platform cannot issue a distinct identity; it also adds a dependency that needs monitoring and recovery planning. If separation must wait, narrow the access only if the platform can enforce and log that scope; otherwise, keep the full reach visible and agree the supplier-supported change path.

**Worked choice: gateway interim or target-native identities.** The shared-account facts establish edge and partial core reach, but do not establish whether the target supports separate identities or whether a gateway is available. For an educational comparison, assume the target cannot be separated in the current change window, while an operator-controlled gateway could restrict requests but has not been tested. No gateway deployment, cost or service outcome is a case fact.

Keeping the shared account avoids another dependency and preserves current operations, but leaves cross-domain reach and weak attribution. A gateway can impose an interim target and command boundary sooner, yet every relevant route must pass through it. Policy errors, direct routes or a supplier path that bypasses it defeat the restriction. A failed gateway can stop legitimate access if it fails closed; fail-open behavior can restore the broad path. A separate target-native identity can make enforcement and attribution easier to verify when the platform supports and logs it, but depends on supplier compatibility, migration effort, a safe window and a rollback that does not quietly restore shared access.

Under the assumption above, consider the gateway only as an interim control if a bounded test proves the allowed edge workflow, denied core access, no alternate route, attributable target logs, revocation and safe behavior on gateway loss. Fail closed for automated access, and test a separate, monitored emergency path so the NOC can maintain or restore essential service without an invisible bypass. The service owner confirms impact and fallback; Engineering, IAM and the supplier estimate configuration, monitoring, support, test and backfill effort. No cash estimate is available until those work packages are scoped.

Prefer target-native identities as the durable change once the platform and supplier support them. If gateway tests fail, its behavior cannot be observed, or emergency access cannot be controlled, do not count it as a reduction in reach. Keep continued use of the shared account within its existing scope only under an authorized, time-bounded service decision; if the service cannot tolerate that reach, use a tested restriction or manual path and escalate under Chapter 5 while the supported migration is prepared. Reopen the interim choice if a direct route appears, the gateway’s availability changes, the target gains native identity support, or service dependencies alter. Verify the final target permissions and logs after migration; a gateway rule or successful configuration push alone is not proof.

Treat the edge fix as a migration, not just an access edit. Map dependent workflows and agree the supplier change and rollback before the window; use the gate below to decide whether the service is ready and whether the new boundary is proven.

**Choose the cutover sequence.** The case establishes broad shared-account reach, supplier configuration work and a safe-window dependency. It does not establish whether the target supports parallel identities, whether each workflow can move separately, or whether rollback would restore the broad account. Provisioning a new identity first and testing it alongside the old one can expose missing permissions before cutover, but leaves both paths available during the overlap and can confuse operators if credentials or logs are not distinct. Disabling the old account first reduces that overlap, but a failure in the new path may interrupt supplier support or monitoring. Moving workflows one at a time can limit the blast radius, but takes more engineering and NOC coordination and may leave a partially migrated estate.

Prefer staged preparation when the supplier and target support it: test the new identity and denied paths in a safe environment, move one bounded workflow at a time, and set a locally justified end point for any temporary overlap. The NOC must know which identity each workflow uses and how to observe it; IAM must verify that the old account is disabled and its sessions ended after cutover. A rollback that reactivates the broad account is not a neutral return to the previous state. Treat it as a separate, time-bounded service-risk decision with an owner, monitoring, expiry and escalation path. If no safe rollback preserves essential visibility, do not start production cutover until an alternate service path is tested. Estimate the extra credential management, supplier effort, test cycles, NOC hand-offs and window duration; none is established by the case.

Permissions matter only if teams can contain a failure without losing the surrounding service. That takes rehearsal.

## Move 3: Rehearsal tests the response before an incident

A policy alone may not tell the night shift who can revoke a compromised identity. A tabletop can expose that gap before an incident does. Its value is in testing decisions and service impact, not demonstrating an AI product.

Choose the exercise form from the unresolved question. A tabletop tests who can decide, what service is affected and which fallback is credible; it cannot prove that a connector or target will block an action. A bounded technical validation tests revocation, existing sessions, downstream calls and monitoring, but cannot show whether teams coordinate under pressure. Use the least disruptive format that can answer the decision. Rehearse the full hand-off only when the technical boundary and roles must be tested together and the service risk justifies the added coordination. That needs SOC/NOC and service-owner time, possible supplier support, backfill and a safe environment or supported window. Do not turn a policy question into a live-core experiment. If no safe technical test exists, keep the boundary unverified, restrict action authority and prepare manual fallback. Reopen the exercise when service, dependency or control changes.

Bring the SOC and NOC, identity and platform owners, service assurance, change management and business continuity into the exercise. Legal, communications, procurement and a supplier contact can join when the scenario needs them. Center it on a decision that could affect a service.

A rehearsal can make the authority boundary explicit. The operator can pre-authorize suspension of a dedicated agent identity under defined conditions, while requiring service-owner input when revocation would also remove NOC visibility or supplier-case handling. That distinction is not a reason to delay containment when misuse is confirmed. Start the fallback with the containment action; the NOC should not have to choose under pressure between leaving access active and making a broad change.

The Canadian Centre for Cyber Security recommends practising for incidents that may unfold within hours, with isolation steps approved in advance and manual alternatives tested for critical functions. CISA, ASD ACSC and partner agencies’ joint guidance also warns that external content can carry instructions designed to steer an agent, a risk known as indirect prompt injection. These are general recommendations. Operators can test them against their own systems and response roles.

A separate CISA CI Fortify tabletop package presents a hypothetical telecommunications outage and asks teams to examine isolation, third-party dependencies and manual operations. It is a U.S. critical-infrastructure exercise template, not an AI-specific scenario or a reported incident. Operators can add an agent identity or tool failure to test those continuity decisions.

CISA, FBI and international partners’ September 2026 guidance for service providers emphasizes clear, timely outage communications that preserve operational security and containment. An exercise can clarify who approves an update, when it is sent and which backup channel remains if containment changes service availability.

Set success criteria before inviting teams: which access must stop, what NOC visibility and fallback must remain, who can authorize containment, and which local response or recovery tolerances apply. A fast revocation is not a pass if it hides alarms or removes the only recovery path. Keep the exercise to one critical service domain and separate technical validation from live production changes. Record decision times, service impact, missing evidence, fallback and manual work against local tolerances, not a universal target.

**Turn each exercise finding into a different management action.** A single “exercise passed/failed” label is too coarse. A technical failure, an unclear decision right, an unavailable fallback and a missing supplier record do not need the same fix, and counting findings gives no priority.

- If a prohibited request reaches the target, or revocation leaves a live session able to act, treat the boundary as failed for that path. Restrict or pause the affected action using an authorized service-safe route, preserve the evidence needed to understand the path, and assign the target or identity owner to correct and retest it. Do not wait for a policy rewrite to compensate for an enforcement failure.
- If participants cannot identify who may suspend the identity, the gap is decision authority. Name a primary and deputy, define the service conditions for pre-authorized containment and specify when the service owner must join. Retest the hand-off; a new RACI document is not proof that an off-hours operator can reach the decision-maker.
- If revocation removes essential monitoring or supplier-case handling, the gap is continuity. The service owner identifies an alternative view or manual method, and the NOC tests how it is activated and staffed. If misuse is confirmed, containment still proceeds through the incident authority; the fallback is part of the response, not a reason to leave confirmed misuse active.
- If the needed supplier evidence is unavailable, record the exact claim it prevents the operator from verifying, the accountable supplier contact and a date or event for review. Narrow the workflow to an observable boundary or keep it restricted. Do not convert an unanswered request into a presumed control failure, but do not count supplier assurance as a passed operator test either.

Rank findings by service consequence, whether the current path can cause that consequence, and how quickly a tested interim treatment can reduce it. A low-consequence documentation gap may wait for the normal roadmap; an unbounded action path on a critical service may require immediate restriction and escalation. The exercise lead records the owner, due trigger, evidence needed to close and service effect of any interim treatment. Close an item only when the specified evidence exists or the delegated executive makes an authorized, time-bounded decision. This turns rehearsal into a funded work queue without claiming that this composite exercise found any particular defect.

## Move 4: A funded roadmap beats a collection of tools

The first three moves reveal gaps in authority, control effectiveness and response. Leaders can use those findings to decide what to fund and what risk they can accept. A credible board paper names the service at risk, proposed work, required resources and remaining risk.

A 12-month roadmap should hold three or four priorities, not every desirable control. Give each a sponsor, delivery lead, target window, dependencies, cost estimate and decision gate. Show supplier and legacy-system constraints. Explain the cost of delay in operator terms: customer-data exposure, reduced network control, slower containment, missed duties or lost service.

Priority should follow service risk, not AI novelty. Compare affected services, identity/tool reach, control effectiveness and the safety of change. A high-impact gap with a long supplier dependency may need an interim treatment and an accelerated supplier plan; a lower-impact use with a reversible change may wait. Make the basis visible without pretending one score can rank every use.

**Compare competing work with the same evidence.** When fixes compete for engineering capacity or a maintenance window, use four questions before ranking them:

| Question | Evidence to bring | Trade-off the decision must show |
|---|---|---|
| What service and path are exposed? | Affected service, identity, reachable systems or data, existing controls and gaps. Mark unverified reach as unknown. | Service consequence if the path is misused or stays open. |
| What risk does the treatment reduce? | The boundary changed and a test or observation of the result. | Prevention, detection, containment or recovery; do not count them as equivalent. |
| What does implementation cost? | Engineering/test effort, supplier charges, backfill and window needs, transition/exit cost; show estimate basis and uncertainty. | Risk reduced versus displaced work, lost diagnostic value, recurring dependency and new operational risk. |
| What remains open? | Supplier commitment, supported procedure, rollback, fallback and reassessment trigger. | Whether exposure fits appetite and delegation, or must be escalated before deferral. |

When two control changes need the same supported window, combining them may reduce repeat service disturbance and supplier call-outs. It also makes fault isolation and rollback harder if one step fails. Separate windows provide a clean observation point but use more NOC and engineering capacity and may leave one exposure open longer. Combine only when each step has an independent success test and can be rolled back without undoing the other; otherwise, sequence by verified service risk and keep the second within a tested interim boundary. Confirm dependencies, rollback and the post-change observation period with Engineering, NOC, the service owner and supplier. Reorder if the first change alters the second’s test conditions, the service tolerance changes or the interim control fails.

**Set the pilot’s scale-up gate before seeing the result.** A supplier demonstration or faster generated answer is not enough to justify broader deployment. The existing NOC-assistant case establishes summaries and draft ticket updates, but no time saving, quality result or service outcome. Before a bounded pilot, the service owner should define the task cohort, current workflow baseline, acceptable service effect and what counts as an operator-accepted result. Security and Operations define the control conditions: permitted data and identity, source traceability, target denial of disallowed actions, review and fallback. Choose local acceptance thresholds before the trial; do not invent a universal accuracy or time-saving percentage.

**Choose the pilot mode for the question you need answered.** These modes provide different evidence; “pilot” is not one standard test.

- **Synthetic validation** is useful for confirming that an identity, connector or target refuses prohibited requests and that missing, stale or conflicting inputs are handled as intended. It limits exposure of real service data and lets teams repeat edge cases. It does not establish performance on live operational records, supplier processing behavior with real data or service impact.
- **Historical replay** can compare summaries against known cases and expose review, correction and source-coverage needs. It also requires careful selection and protection of records; a replay may omit the timing, changing dependencies and data freshness of a live incident. The data owner must approve the use, and the evaluation must preserve the original decision context.
- **Shadow processing** runs the assistant beside the existing NOC process without letting it change the service. It can reveal live timing, gaps and operator correction effort, but the model and connectors still process data, and a “no action” label does not prove there are no side effects. Minimize data, confirm supplier and retention boundaries, keep outputs clearly separate from operational instructions, and stop if the shadow path degrades visibility or service.
- **Bounded live read/draft use** tests the task in real workflow conditions, but consumes reviewer and backfill capacity and may create reliance before the evidence is strong. Use it only after the identity and data boundary, independent stop path, source provenance and manual fallback are verified; keep proposals inside normal change control.

**Test the target boundary, not just the simulation.** A mock target or test tenant can confirm that the assistant formats a request or that a mock policy rejects it. It does not prove that the production network element, ticketing connector or identity provider enforces the same restriction. For every test result, state what was real: the principal, connector, policy engine, target, logging path and revocation mechanism. A test that uses a different identity or a simulated target is evidence about the component it exercised, not proof of the end-to-end production boundary.

There are three choices when the production target cannot safely be used for a negative test. Accept the sandbox result as sufficient and enable the action; this is quick but leaves the production enforcement assumption unverified. Keep the target permission denied and validate the route through a controlled staged test at the real target, using a non-service-affecting action if one exists; this costs engineering and target-owner time but produces more direct evidence. Or retain read/draft behavior and defer write authority until such a test can be safely supported; this gives up immediate automation but does not convert a simulator result into a production claim. Under an educational assumption that the supplier’s test tenant does not share the production target’s authorization path, choose the third route for any service-impacting write. A staged target test can change that recommendation only when the target owner verifies the exact principal, allowed and denied outcomes, logs, active-session behavior, stop path and recovery evidence.

For the composite, a proportionate sequence is to test enforcement with synthetic requests, assess source behavior using approved historical or safely replayed material, then consider shadow or bounded read/draft use if the service owner needs live evidence. Skip a mode only when the decision it would answer is not relevant; do not use one safe result to claim a different kind of assurance. If the operator cannot approve the data handling or prevent side effects for live processing, remain in synthetic or approved replay and report that production behavior is still unverified.

There are three defensible outcomes. Expand on a positive average result and accept more scope quickly; this is fastest, but can hide high-consequence errors or additional review work. Keep the pilot bounded while collecting results across representative task and service conditions; this delays benefit and consumes NOC, engineering and supplier capacity, but can expose variation and corrections. Stop or return to manual work when quality, service impact or effort misses the owner’s threshold; this avoids scaling a weak process but gives up the possible efficiency benefit. Review the tail cases and correction effort, not only the mean response time, and keep the cost of review, rework, supplier charges and displaced work in the comparison.

Test source coverage as a separate scale-up condition, not as one more score in an average quality result. A summary can be well written and still be unsafe if one market feed is missing, its timestamp is stale, or two operational records conflict. Chapter 2 explains how to represent coverage and provenance; here, the decision is whether the NOC may rely on the assistant for the task cohort being piloted. Use synthetic or safely replayed inputs to test missing, delayed and contradictory sources. The expected behavior is to show which source is absent or stale, preserve links or identifiers that let the reviewer inspect the originals, and avoid turning an unobserved service area into a region-wide “clear.” If action depends on the missing evidence, the workflow should stop at a draft or request local confirmation rather than infer a safe state.

The service owner chooses whether the pilot may still produce a limited summary with a visible coverage warning, or whether incomplete coverage requires manual confirmation before the summary enters an operational decision. The choice depends on service use: a retrospective trend view may tolerate a disclosed gap; a live incident triage decision may not. Record how often the reviewer must find missing context, correct a stale source or resolve a conflict, and whether the assistant made that limitation visible before the review. If the connectors do not expose source freshness or completeness, the model cannot reliably reconstruct it from prose. Keep that workflow bounded until the data owner or platform supplies an enforceable coverage signal.

**Treat reviewer disagreement as evidence, not noise.** An “accepted” summary can still contain an unsafe omission if one shift catches it and another does not. A single acceptance rate hides whether reviewers agreed on the underlying service facts, merely accepted the wording, or lacked time to inspect a source. Record correction and override reasons by task type: missing evidence, stale data, wrong service attribution, unsupported inference, unsafe suggested action or presentation defect. Preserve a sample of the original input, output and reviewer decision under approved retention controls so a later review can distinguish model error from ambiguous source data or an unclear local procedure.

If qualified reviewers disagree about whether an output is operationally safe, do not average their votes or resolve the matter by tuning the prompt until the answer looks consistent. Ask the service owner or delegated authority to define the reference decision and evidence. When that reference does not exist, keep the workflow at draft and use the pilot to identify the missing service rule. If the disagreement is about a cosmetic issue, correct the interface; if it concerns impact, authority or evidence sufficiency, hold the scale-up gate. Compare this pattern across shifts and task classes, and check the review burden as well as the output. Agreement among reviewers is useful only when they had the same adequate evidence and authority.

Under the current facts, do not scale beyond the bounded read/draft task until both the service result and the authority boundary pass. If output quality is acceptable but review effort cancels the gain, revise the workflow or stop; if the result helps but the target boundary fails, keep authority restricted. A passing pilot supports only the tested task, data, identity and service conditions. Expand one dimension at a time—users, data scope or action rights—and retest the changed boundary before relying on it. No pilot outcome is established here.

**Make the transition to routine service ownership a separate decision.** A pilot can show that one cohort benefits under a staffed test; it does not establish that the NOC can support the capability across shifts, service changes, supplier updates or incident conditions. Before handoff, name both the technical owner who maintains the identity, connector, model and logs and the service owner who decides acceptable impact and fallback. The operating team needs a supported procedure for access changes, failure, service degradation, supplier escalation, evidence retention and return to the manual workflow. Budget recurring supplier, engineering and review effort, not only the pilot’s setup cost.

Before routine use, confirm that day, night and weekend teams can see source provenance, identify the owner, pause the workflow, invoke the fallback and reach supplier support within local service tolerance. The service owner approves the task boundary and conditions; the platform and security teams maintain technical controls; the change authority approves any production modification. Reassess when the model or supplier changes, new data or connectors are added, user volume exceeds tested review capacity, or the workflow moves to another market or service. Retest only the changed claims, but do not carry forward evidence that no longer describes the deployed path. If there is no funded owner or operational handoff, the pilot remains a pilot; it should not become production by inertia.

**Keep the fallback operational, not ceremonial.** A manual NOC process can lose readiness if it is invoked rarely: staff rotate, runbooks refer to removed screens, permissions expire, or the queue has grown around the assistant’s capacity. The choice is not simply to keep or remove the fallback. Full manual capability is resilient but consumes ongoing staff and practice time. A thinner on-call capability costs less day to day but may fail under a broad incident or concurrent service demand. Removing the fallback simplifies operations and increases dependency on the assistant, supplier and connector being available and trustworthy.

For the NOC-assistant composite, retain a practiced manual route for the bounded task until the service owner accepts an alternative and the operator has demonstrated it under the relevant shift conditions. Exercise a realistic transition: the assistant becomes unavailable or untrusted, the duty team identifies which outputs or draft tickets need validation, uses an approved source and workflow, and reaches the right service owner without relying on the same connector. Measure the additional workload and service delay against local tolerance. If the team cannot perform the fallback within that tolerance, do not declare handoff complete; fund backfill and procedure repair, narrow the supported task, or keep the pilot staffed. Retest after material changes to the workflow or staffing model. A written fallback is an instruction; only a current, staffed and exercised path is an operating capability.

A manual route is not independent if it uses the same identity, connector or supplier path that has been paused. Under an educational assumption that the assistant’s service identity is suspected, the NOC should not sign into the manual workflow with that same principal simply because its password still works. Reusing a shared break-glass credential restores availability quickly but weakens attribution and may leave broad access active after the incident. A separately governed emergency role can preserve continuity, but it must be protected, time-bounded, logged and tested under the operator’s approval rules. The service owner and IAM team should verify what operations it permits, who can invoke it, how it is revoked and what evidence remains when ordinary logging is impaired. If no independent, supported route exists, say so in the continuity plan and fund one; do not claim the fallback is available based on a procedure that depends on the failed boundary.

If the emergency role is invoked, treat the event as a controlled transition, not a return to normal by itself. Reconcile actions taken, confirm service visibility and ticket state, revoke the temporary permission, and have the delegated change authority decide when ordinary access can resume. Review the event before closing the exception; a clean restoration check must not erase evidence that was needed to investigate. Retest the emergency route after changes to the identity provider, target permissions or on-call rota, and after any invocation that reveals a gap.

The shared edge/core account and deferred patch window show why a simple ranking is not yet defensible. The account reaches edge nodes and parts of the core; a supplier change and safe window are needed. A fixed patch release exists, but the next suitable window is three weeks away. These facts do not establish comparative service impact, interim-control effectiveness or effort. Do not rank by labels such as “core” or by release availability; obtain the missing impact, fallback, support and effort evidence.

**When capacity permits only one change.** If staffing or a supported window allows only one change this cycle, compare affected services and fallback, reach and threat, supported options, supplier lead time, rollback, effort and deferral cost. Mark each point verified, assumed or unknown. A temporary restriction counts only when a safe test shows its effect; monitoring is not prevention.

Escalate any exposure outside appetite or beyond delegated authority, and show which other work would move. If both remain within appetite, sequence them using tested interim controls, continuity impact and deferral cost, then record the review trigger through Chapter 5’s decision process. Missing critical evidence keeps the choice open; a booked window is not remediation.

An interim treatment should name the path it is meant to constrain and the evidence that it does so. A narrower identity may reduce reach; additional monitoring may help detect misuse but does not prevent access. Those controls have different effects and should not be reported as equivalent. If a restriction slows diagnostics or shifts work back to manual operations, record that service cost alongside the exposure it reduces.

Measures should show whether risk is changing, not how many tools were purchased. Useful indicators include named owners and data paths for high-impact uses, tested access revocation, clear containment authority, exercised service alternatives and closed exercise findings. A tool count can rise while access and response stay the same.

For each indicator, give the board a denominator, evidence date and service scope. “Access reviews completed” is weak if it does not show which identities were reviewed, which target permissions were tested, and what exceptions remain. “Exercise actions closed” is weak if the action has not been re-tested or if it closes a finding without restoring the service owner’s fallback. Report service and market coverage separately where a shared platform does not have one uniform boundary; do not let strong evidence for one region conceal an untested one elsewhere.

Pair leading evidence with service outcomes. The first can show whether identities, connectors, suppliers and target permissions are governed and tested; the second can show whether the NOC detects a failure, makes a safe decision and maintains service under the conditions actually exercised. A favorable trend in one does not substitute for a failure in the other. For the NOC assistant, track task and source coverage, material corrections, review effort, action-boundary test results and the availability of manual fallback. For the shared account, track confirmed reach, denied cross-domain attempts and tested revocation; for a patch decision, track version/reach confirmation, the state of any interim treatment and the next supported route. These are evidence categories, not universal thresholds or claims that any test has passed.

Set the decision threshold with the service owner before reporting the result. If target denial or fallback fails, restrict the path and remediate even when other measures improve. If evidence is stale or incomplete, report that as unknown rather than averaging it into a green score. If all material conditions remain within tolerance, continue the funded plan and retest on change. The board should see which movement changes a decision: what would trigger restriction, escalation, additional capacity or a controlled expansion.

A policy is not proof. For high-impact uses, board assurance should focus on observed control behavior under service conditions, independent review where justified, and unresolved service impact. A purchase, policy or successful staging test alone does not show that the production boundary works.

Regulation belongs in this roadmap, but it need not delay action. Legal and Compliance can track binding obligations by market and separate them from consultation drafts. Where duties overlap with identity, supplier assurance, incident reporting or continuity, existing governance may be enough; a separate AI approval board is not always needed.

At an executive review, the CISO and CTO should connect each funding or change-window request to the exposure it will reduce, the work it will displace and the evidence that will show progress. State what would reopen the choice if delivery slips or conditions change.

**Deferred patch window.** The next safe window is three weeks away. The roadmap must show the service cost of an earlier change and the exposure of waiting, then identify evidence that would reopen the choice if threat, interim controls or service conditions change.

Chapter 3 separates threat and reach checks from change readiness. Apply that distinction here. The fixed release and three-week window are the known timing facts; they do not establish whether the deployed live-core version is affected, whether the relevant path is reachable, or whether an interim restriction works. Security and platform teams verify version and reach. The control owner and NOC test any restriction and its effect on monitoring and service. The service owner supplies impact and fallback evidence. Engineering and the supplier confirm the earliest supported window, test requirements and rollback. A public threat signal can raise urgency, but it cannot prove local exposure or make an unsupported live-core change safe.

Compare three routes. Ask the supplier and Engineering to bring forward the earliest supported change; it can shorten exposure but needs capacity, may incur expedite costs and can displace other work. Keep the scheduled window and rely only on controls already shown to reduce the reachable path; this preserves planned test and rollback conditions but leaves some exposure and may consume NOC/SOC effort or restrict a service function. Temporarily narrow or suspend affected access only if a Level 3 test demonstrates that the restriction denies the relevant path under tested conditions and preserves an adequate fallback; this can reduce reach sooner but may disrupt dependent services. Estimate supplier, engineering, test, backfill and rollback effort from local evidence; no cost figure is established here.

On the available facts, ask the supplier and Engineering for the earliest supported change, but do not execute it early until local version, reach and service impact justify the added cost and the test/rollback path remains sound. Keep the scheduled window as the fallback. During the wait, rely on an interim restriction only after a Level 3 test shows that the relevant path is denied under test conditions while required service visibility remains available. If no effective restriction exists, do not describe waiting as controlled. Bring the change forward when local evidence confirms a reachable affected version or credible threat evidence materially raises urgency, and the supplier can support the earlier path safely. If no safe earlier path exists and the service cannot tolerate continued exposure, escalate for a service-specific restriction. Any remaining exposure needs the Chapter 5 decision record; escalate if it exceeds appetite or no executive has authority over the affected services.

Reopen the choice if the deployed version or reachable path changes, a restriction fails, supplier support changes, the window slips, new threat evidence changes urgency or service impact/fallback changes. Before and after the change, verify the targeted version, denied access path, attributable logs, monitoring, service health and rollback. Until the supported change is applied and those results are recorded, a booked window is a schedule commitment, not risk reduction.

**Illustrative planning sequence for the next 12 months.** These are decision stages, not prescribed dates or universal deadlines. Place them against the operator’s change calendar, supplier commitments and service priorities. Completing a deliverable is not evidence of risk reduction.

| Stage | Lead, decision and dependency | Evidence required to proceed or close |
|---|---|---|
| **Establish the baseline** | Security and service/platform teams map supplier-feature, NOC-assistant and edge-account paths; gather supplier, product and identity records. | Known/unknown identity, data, actions and reach; flag immediate restriction needs. A baseline is not proof of control. |
| **Before widening authority** | Identity/platform, Network Engineering, NOC and supplier plan boundaries and edge migration; confirm test capacity and a supported window. | Configuration review, allow/deny tests, attributable logs, revocation. Without safe testing, retain interim controls and mark the boundary unverified. |
| **Before relying on containment** | Incident-response lead with SOC/NOC, service assurance, change management and supplier rehearses the NOC assistant and fallback; map identities first. | Local decision/timing results plus technical revocation and visibility tests. Keep tabletop claims separate from technical evidence. |
| **At portfolio review** | CTO/CISO sponsors and resource leads sequence work and capacity; patch and edge migration need supported windows. | Separate delivery from tested risk reduction; report impact, tests, dependencies and triggers. Count patching after effectiveness checks. |

Before agreeing a stage, estimate network and identity engineering, NOC/SOC backfill, supplier work, test environments and change windows. Show the estimate basis and which work would move; if unknown, mark it pending rather than inventing a budget or implying that the work costs nothing.

**Decide how to handle work that displaces the plan.** A roadmap that assumes every engineering hour and supplier window will remain available is not credible in a live network. One option is to protect capacity for incident-driven work and supplier escalations; another is to schedule fully and agree in advance which roadmap item slips when urgent work consumes that capacity. A reserve has an opportunity cost. Full utilization can delay a control change and extend exposure. Use local evidence: on-call load, maintenance windows, supplier lead times and the history of work displaced by urgent changes. Do not invent a universal reserve percentage. Name the stage that pauses, its interim treatment and the service condition that requires escalation. Revisit the choice when a dependency or service condition changes.

Reopen a stage when a dependency fails, access expands, a test fails or service conditions change. Shared identities or supplier paths that cross service domains need a decision at the authority boundary; escalate when delegated authority does not cover the full impact.
