# Chapter 3: The Threat Moves Faster Than the Operator Can Respond

Fast detection is not fast response. The SOC may see an alert while the NOC sees a planned change; IAM may be able to revoke a credential, and a supplier may hold platform details. Without a clear decision path, an attacker may retain access while the operator checks service impact.

## Threat activity can accelerate without being fully autonomous

The Canadian Centre for Cyber Security’s 26 October 2026 bulletin is cross-sector evidence, not a telecom incident or a universal attack timeline. It says attacks are not yet fully autonomous: humans still choose targets, set objectives and oversee operations. For some activity attributed by the bulletin to Storm-0940, the time from target selection to privileged cloud access was less than three hours.

In a 15 October 2026 advisory, CERT-In reported that tools using AI agents shortened the time from initial access to impact from days to a few hours in a financially motivated campaign against Indian entities. The advisory did not identify those organizations as telecom operators; it also reported parallel agent operations and repeat attacks within days after incomplete remediation.

A separate 4 November advisory from CERT-In’s finance-sector incident-response team reported attacks against Indian BFSI entities timed to holidays, weekends and other low-monitoring windows, including after incomplete remediation. This financial-sector evidence supports testing off-hours decision authority and escalation, not a claim about telco incidents.

Neither report establishes an operator response deadline. Faster adversary activity does not make every emergency change safe on a live service.

Chapter 1’s live-core patch decision supplies a second clock: the fixed version is known, but the supported change is three weeks away. The three-week interval is specific to this patch decision, not a universal benchmark. Separate time to confirm affected services and interim protections from the lead time for a production change; they call for different actions. If exposure changes, reassess interim protection and change readiness; an unsupported emergency change is not automatically safer.

During an active review, keep four tracks moving in parallel. Security and platform owners verify the deployed version, reachable path and affected services. The control owner and NOC/service assurance verify any interim restriction and its effect on monitoring, supplier access and customer work. Engineering and the supplier confirm the fixed release, earliest supported window, testing and rollback. The service owner supplies impact evidence; any decision to continue exposure must be time-bounded, within appetite and made by an executive whose delegation covers every affected service. Each track has separate closure evidence. A window date is not mitigation, and a known fixed version does not prove local exposure. Reopen if threat, reach, control result or service conditions change.

Detection and response are different capabilities. A dashboard can show an event, but people still need authority and context to decide whether to revoke an identity, isolate a host, block a supplier connection or keep a service running in reduced mode.

## Response runs on several timelines

Incident response is often described as a technical sequence: detect, investigate, contain, recover. In practice, workstreams overlap. The NOC checks whether an alarm matches a real network change; the SOC assesses whether the activity is malicious; IAM may need to disable an account. Service assurance estimates customer and service impact, while legal, regulatory and communications teams work against their own deadlines.

At the same time, the network cannot wait for every team to finish its analysis. Nor can the operator treat every system as disposable. A management platform may support several network domains. An identity may be shared across automation jobs. A supplier connection may be needed to restore a service even as the supplier helps investigate it. Availability changes the containment decision.

It helps to separate four response intervals:

- **Detection:** from the first relevant signal to recognition that the activity may be malicious.
- **Decision:** from that recognition to an authorized containment choice.
- **Action:** from the decision to safe execution and confirmation of the result.
- **Continuity and recovery:** from containment to the safe restoration of affected services.

A fast SOC does not guarantee a fast response if approval is slow. Quick isolation is not success if it breaks a critical workflow or removes telemetry needed to understand the incident. Assess each interval against the service’s agreed continuity tolerance; the same target will not fit every domain. Record service impact and the reason for each delay as well as elapsed time. An overall response figure alone cannot show whether the constraint was missing evidence, unclear authority, a supplier dependency or an untested fallback.

Use the SOC/NOC exercise to locate the delay: missing alert context, unclear decision authority, technical execution or fallback.

For each unresolved gap, name the interim treatment, the supplier or engineering work needed and the retest. A response target that rewards speed alone can encourage unsafe isolation. If no tested option meets the service tolerance, record the shortfall and require a time-bounded decision from the delegated service executive before exposure continues; escalate if authority or appetite is exceeded. Do not mark the response “on target.”

**Set an objective for safe containment, not one network-wide clock.** A single target is simple to report, but can count an unsafe isolation as success and hide different service tolerances. No target avoids a false promise but leaves leaders unable to see which delay needs investment. Service-specific objectives take more work to define and rehearse; they distinguish detection, decision, action and recovery, and state what service view or function must remain available.

For an exercise using the existing NOC assistant, assume the broad account can be revoked only as a whole and no independent alarm-read path has been verified. A dashboard that measures only time to disable the account could show a fast response while leaving the NOC without the visibility needed to manage the network. Treat threat-speed reports as stress cases for exercises, not as a universal service deadline.

Under that assumption, count containment as successful only when the target blocks the disallowed path, relevant sessions are stopped or bounded, and the NOC keeps its alarm view or a tested fallback. Measure the chain from first signal through recognition, authorized decision and target-confirmed action to safe restoration. The service owner sets the tolerated delay; Security, IAM and the NOC supply evidence about control behavior and service visibility. If every available stop removes essential visibility, the problem is the design, not the response clock.

Fund the exercise needed to establish the baseline: NOC/SOC backfill, supplier participation, identity and platform engineering, and logs that connect the signal to the target result. Test off-hours and divided attention, then report elapsed time together with service impact, manual work and the reason for delay. Keep statutory reporting deadlines separate from internal containment objectives. If independent alarm visibility and narrow revocation are later tested, the operator may pre-authorize a faster bounded action. If those conditions remain unproven, invest in the safe path before tightening the clock. The objective changes when the service, fallback or tested control changes, not simply when a threat report describes a faster attacker.

CERT-In’s April 2026 advisory recommends tabletop exercises for five simultaneous incidents rather than a single incident, explicitly modelling AI-driven scenarios. That recommendation is Indian cross-sector guidance, not a telco standard or universal target. For an operator, the test is whether NOC, SOC, IAM and service teams can still make timely, service-safe decisions when their attention is split.

**Choose a command model for shared dependencies.** When an exercise introduces simultaneous incidents that touch a shared management platform or compete for the same NOC, engineering or supplier capacity, the operator can use one central incident lead, separate incident teams, or a federated model. One lead can align priorities and preserve a common view, but can become a decision bottleneck and may not know each service’s tolerance. Separate teams can move locally, but may isolate a shared platform or consume the same scarce specialist twice. A federated model uses a cross-service coordinator for common priorities and resource conflicts, while named service leads supply impact and fallback evidence and act only within pre-authorized limits. It needs explicit arbitration and deputies; otherwise, it adds another hand-off without resolving the conflict.

For a service provider, the point is not to centralize every technical action. The coordinator can maintain the shared incident picture, coordinate scarce engineering and supplier support within existing delegation, and flag competing changes. Each service lead explains what isolation would remove and which fallback is ready. If a decision crosses services or exceeds an owner’s delegation, the service executive with authority across the affected services decides or escalates. A central incident role does not gain authority to accept service risk merely by coordinating the response. Prioritize by service consequence and tested alternatives, not by the number of alerts or a single severity label.

Exercise the model with actual on-call roles, deputies and supplier contacts, including the shift when the primary decision-maker is unavailable. Record which decision waited for a common view, which waited for service evidence, and which waited for people or supplier capacity; also record displaced maintenance and any tolerance exceeded. If coordination caused delay, change the decision route; if the limit was coverage, fund on-call and backfill; if the conflict was shared technical dependency, define who arbitrates the change and what fallback remains. Do not grant broad action authority as a substitute for missing staff or cross-service delegation.

## The SK Telecom response had distinct timelines

The South Korean Ministry of Science and ICT (MSIT) investigation into SK Telecom’s 2025 breach shows that response includes more than containment. MSIT reports that SK Telecom detected abnormally large outbound traffic at 11:20 p.m. on 18 April 2025 and notified KISA at 4:46 p.m. on 20 April; the Ministry said this exceeded the statutory 24-hour reporting window.

That is the reporting clock, not the containment clock. The public-private investigation team was established on 23 April and inspected all 42,605 SK Telecom servers through 27 June. Its final findings identified 28 infected servers. The inspection end date does not tell us when containment occurred; keep notification, containment and forensic scoping separate.

**Run reporting clocks beside the technical response.** The SK Telecom timeline shows why notification needs its own owner and record. When an event may affect services or data across markets, waiting for complete forensic scope can delay a required notice, while one broad early statement can overstate impact or conflict with local duties. A single global “incident confirmed” time may not show when each applicable reporting obligation begins. Legal and Compliance must verify the trigger, clock and recipient for each relevant service, data type and jurisdiction rather than infer them from another market’s rule.

Prepare a factual notification path before an incident: who maintains the event timeline, who checks each duty, who approves the message, and who can send it if the primary lead is unavailable. Preserve separately the first signal, the point at which the event met each applicable threshold, containment actions and customer impact. When a notice is required before scope is complete, report what is confirmed, what remains unknown, the service or data categories under review, the protective action already taken and when the next update will follow. Do not wait for attribution if it is not needed to meet the applicable duty, and do not describe a suspected effect as confirmed. Local legal review, translation, regulator contacts and customer-care capacity all consume time; estimate and exercise them rather than treating a template as readiness.

The customer response had its own operational demands. In its preliminary findings, the Ministry encouraged subscribers to replace their USIM cards or use a protection service, and urged the operator to expand service channels and add a reservation system so more customers could be protected promptly.
These reports describe a breach-response timeline, not a network outage. They show how an incident can create a second workload: protecting customers, handling enquiries and changing business operations while the technical investigation continues.

The customer-protection choice runs alongside forensic scoping. Waiting for confirmation of every affected record may preserve accuracy but delay useful action; speaking earlier can help customers, but a broad or vague message may drive enquiries before the operator can say who is affected. For a service provider, contact and replacement channels need capacity, not just approved text. Use a staged, factual notice when evidence and applicable duties support customer action: state the affected service or data categories, what remains unknown, what the customer should do and how updates will come. Legal and regulatory leads verify thresholds; customer operations estimate and exercise contact demand; technical teams supply scope and confidence. Revisit the message as facts or duties change. A notice does not contain an attacker, and it must not imply broader impact than the evidence supports.

**Test the communication path under service failure.** If an outage affects the operator’s app, messaging or data service, a notice sent only through that service may not reach affected customers. An external status page or partner channel may be reachable but has its own availability, authentication and update dependencies. Contact centres and retail channels can reach customers through staff, but may saturate and displace other service work. No single channel should be assumed to reach every affected customer.

Choose a layered set of channels from the services and markets at risk, applicable notification duties, customer preferences and tested reach. Name who can activate each channel, where the approved facts come from, and how an update stays consistent if one system is unavailable. The message should distinguish confirmed impact, unknown scope and next update time; a usable channel does not justify a broader claim than the evidence supports. Test the plan by making the primary channel unavailable in an exercise, then measure whether the alternate route can be activated, authenticated and staffed. Include customer-care capacity, language or market needs, supplier dependencies and the work displaced. If alternate reach is unverified, report that as a continuity gap rather than claiming the communications plan is ready.

CISA, the FBI, and partner agencies recommend a service-outage communications plan with triggers, escalation paths, and templates for status pages, customer and partner notices, and regulatory communications. They advise frequent updates on what is known, unknown and under investigation, while protecting operational security and aligning with law-enforcement and containment efforts. The guidance does not describe the SK Telecom breach; for an operator, it can inform planning when defensive isolation changes service availability.

Assign separate leads for notification, forensic scoping and customer protection so each can move while containment continues.

## Containment is a service decision

Containment decisions at a service provider have more than one consequence. Cutting a compromised supplier connection may slow recovery. Disabling a shared identity may stop a legitimate monitoring workflow. Isolating a management host may reduce an attacker’s access but also remove a source of operational visibility.

**Supplier support during containment.** For the edge/core decision, assume a supplier support session uses the shared account during an incident, while supplier engineers are needed for a supported configuration change. The composite establishes shared edge/core reach and supplier dependence for remediation; the suspected session, misuse and any separate support identity are assumptions, not case facts.

The operator can revoke all supplier access, keep the broad session active under observation, or move to a separate, supervised identity limited to the affected target and task. Full revocation may block new supplier calls, but can delay a supported repair and push work to the NOC; test separately whether it ends existing sessions or queued actions. Keeping access preserves supplier response but does not reduce reach; monitoring alone may not identify which person or agent caused a target action. A separate, time-bounded identity can retain necessary support while limiting scope, but only if the target enforces it, records the supplier principal and closes sessions at expiry. Establishing that path may consume supplier, engineering and NOC time during the incident.

Under the educational assumption, do not keep the broad session merely because the supplier is useful. Set the trigger and fallback from service-owner evidence before the incident; once a pre-authorized trigger is met, use the stop without waiting for full forensic attribution. Continue support only through a separately verified path with a named supplier user, specific target, approved task, operator supervision and expiry. If that path is unavailable or cannot be observed, do not assume manual recovery works: test the local or manual route if one exists. If no safe alternative is available, the authorized service executive decides whether a time-bounded continuation fits service tolerance and delegated authority, or escalates it.

Before relying on the alternate path, confirm at the target that the permitted task succeeds, out-of-scope access fails, the supplier identity appears in the logs and revocation ends existing sessions. The NOC confirms that alarms and service assurance remain available; if a manual route exists, exercise it when the supplier cannot connect. Include supplier response time, engineering effort, NOC backfill and any delayed repair in the decision. If the path or service dependency changes, reassess; a supplier’s statement that access is “supervised” is not evidence of target enforcement.

That does not mean the operator should wait. It means the available actions should be designed and authorized in advance. The Canadian Cyber Centre recommends deciding in advance who may isolate an account, application, network or service, and practising incidents that unfold within hours rather than days. It also recommends identifying essential functions, defining pre-authorized isolation steps and testing safe manual or alternative operations.

Put those criteria in the response playbook: what evidence is enough, which actions the SOC may take alone, when the NOC or service owner must approve, and what fallback keeps essential services running.

A job title on the rota is not a mandate. Define pre-authorized actions by their effect on service, not by tool name. A narrow, reversible restriction on one dedicated identity can be pre-authorized when its trigger, target denial, session behavior and continued alarm view or tested fallback are known. A restriction that affects a network domain or removes supplier support needs the service owner’s impact assessment and approval by an executive with delegated authority for that service. An action on a shared identity or management plane, or one affecting multiple services, needs an executive with authority across them; otherwise, escalate. This reduces delay without giving one role broad authority across services.

For each action, record its trigger, target and scope, maximum duration, expiry, restoration authority and the evidence needed before access returns. If target denial, session termination or NOC visibility cannot be verified, do not pre-authorize that action. Exercise the route with on-call roles and suppliers, including when the primary decision-maker is unavailable; deputies need explicit delegation. Record elapsed time, service impact and fallback result. If the right person cannot act, fix the response design rather than blaming the individual on call.

## The NOC assistant makes authority visible

The NOC assistant introduced in Chapter 1 raises a live response question: can the operator disable its broad service account without losing essential visibility?

Before an incident, verify whether action rights can be stopped independently of alarm reads and vendor-case handling. Name the fallback and who may invoke it. Keep proposed network changes within normal change control.

**Illustrative live-incident decision card: NOC assistant.** Adapt this to the operator’s incident authority and map the role names to local equivalents before use. It is not an authorization by itself, and a tabletop cannot prove that its technical steps work.

| Decision point | What to establish or decide | Who contributes |
|---|---|---|
| Establish scope | Identify principal, tool, source and target; separate request from proposal. Preserve event IDs; protect captures. For persistent memory, identify its read/write principal and scope. | SOC leads assessment; IAM/platform identifies principal and memory path; data owner controls captures; supplier supports provider stores. |
| Bound authority and service | Can action rights stop without losing alarm reads or vendor cases? Do sessions persist? What NOC view is lost, and what fallback remains? | IAM/platform verifies revocation; NOC, service assurance and service owner define impact and fallback. |
| Choose containment | Compare broad revocation, tested narrow limits or controlled continuation. Weigh service impact against risk; note effort, supplier, proof and gaps. | Incident commander coordinates; authorized service executive decides; escalate if authority is missing or insufficient. |
| Verify containment | Confirm downstream denial, attributable logs, fallback and understood service state. | Control operator verifies; NOC/service owner confirm service state; incident lead records action and open questions. |

**Preserve enough evidence without delaying a safe stop.** In a separate response exercise, assume the operator suspects that a NOC-assistant identity or connector was misused. Collecting every prompt and record before containment can delay a pre-authorized stop and expose more customer or topology data than the investigation needs. Disabling all identities immediately may stop the suspected path, but can remove alarm visibility or supplier support and leave sessions or queued work unexamined. Preserve the minimum useful evidence in parallel with containment when safe; do not delay a pre-authorized stop for a complete record.

Before an incident, identify the records that can answer the service decision: originating principal and credential; connector and tool-call identifiers; target request and result; relevant authentication and session state; queued actions; approved source records; and, when persistent context is involved, memory identity and scope. Preserve timestamps and the event identifiers needed to join logs across systems. Use source-system records where available; an assistant-generated summary or supplier assurance statement is not a substitute for the underlying event. The incident lead coordinates the collection, while IAM/platform and the supplier locate their records, the data owner limits captured content, and Legal/Privacy confirm handling and retention requirements. Keep evidence in an approved, access-controlled incident store, separate from operational memory; record who collected it and any transformation. Do not copy whole customer cases or prompts when the decision needs only metadata or a minimal excerpt.

If a pre-authorized stop condition is met, use it without waiting for complete forensic collection. Preserve what is safely available first or in parallel, then verify at the target whether new calls stopped, sessions or queued actions remain, and service visibility continues. If key logs cannot be obtained, mark the action path and incident scope unknown; do not turn that absence into evidence that no action occurred. The capture process itself consumes SOC, IAM, data-owner and supplier time and may require secure storage or forensic support.

A proposed change is not evidence that the target acted. Check connector and target records for any downstream call, active session or queued action; if a pre-authorized stop trigger is already met, use it rather than waiting for full attribution. If no call occurred and a tested restriction blocks the suspected path without removing essential alarm reads, prefer it; include data access and vendor-case submission in scope if they are part of the concern. If a call occurred, sessions persist or evidence is inconclusive, compare broader revocation with an alternate NOC view and manual case handling before stopping the shared identity. If no safe narrow stop or fallback is available, escalate a time-bounded service decision; do not leave broad access in place indefinitely because revocation may affect visibility. Confirm at the target that disallowed calls fail and verify NOC visibility after containment.

Before restoring the capability, a quiet alert stream or completed revocation is not enough. Confirm which principal and sessions stopped and whether a proposed action reached its target; have NOC/service assurance demonstrate essential alarms or an alternate path, then compare restoration with local service tolerance.

**Separate forensic preservation from operational memory.** For the memory decision, assume the NOC assistant used persistent context during a suspected compromise. The composite does not establish that memory was enabled or accessed by an attacker. The operator can reuse it, purge it and rebuild later, or preserve a controlled evidence copy while quarantining it from operational retrieval. Reuse is fastest and avoids re-entry, but can carry stale topology, supplier content or hostile instructions into later work. Purging reduces that path but may destroy evidence and increase NOC workload. Quarantine keeps evidence available to investigators without giving the assistant access to it; the copy remains sensitive and does not authorize operational reuse or supplier transfer.

Under that assumption, preserve the memory state and relevant logs separately under the incident evidence process, then block the live workflow from reading them. Continue only with a service-owner-approved manual path or task-scoped context from authoritative sources; if neither can meet the service need, the authorized service executive decides the temporary service mode. Rebuild persistent context only after the data owner confirms what may be retained and the platform or supplier owner identifies every read/write identity and copy. Estimate NOC backfill, data-owner review, supplier support and rebuild effort locally.

Before operational reuse, verify a permitted retrieval, cross-scope denial and expiry/deletion. Also confirm whether supplier-held logs, indexes or backups retain copies and whether they can be isolated from the runtime. If the assistant can still retrieve quarantined content, or the read/write path cannot be observed, keep the memory out of the workflow. If evidence shows persistent memory was not used, or its scope and clearing are proven, close this step without delaying restoration unnecessarily.

**Stage restoration while investigation continues.** In a restoration exercise, assume the NOC assistant was restricted during an investigation into suspected misuse. The service decision is whether to restore the broad account, keep the whole assistant paused until forensic closure, or restore a minimum verified function while investigation continues. Restoring the broad account reduces manual work fastest but reintroduces reach that has not been shown safe. Waiting for every forensic question to close gives the most time for investigation, but extends manual handling and any degraded service mode; the service owner must make that cost visible. A staged return can preserve useful read/draft work without restoring action rights, but only if the new boundary is independently enforceable and observable.

Before that limited return, confirm that the prior principal is disabled or rotated, relevant downstream sessions and queued actions are ended or bounded, and target tests show the allowed read path and denied action path. Reconcile agent, connector and target logs sufficiently to identify what data and actions may have been reached; preserve the evidence separately from operational context. Keep memory task-scoped or excluded until its read/write scope and clearing are verified. The NOC demonstrates that essential alarm visibility remains, and the service owner confirms the proposed reduced mode fits tolerance. Full actor attribution may remain open, but an unresolved access path, active session or memory route that could reintroduce the suspected authority blocks restoration of that capability.

Under these conditions, restore only the tested read/draft scope, observe it for a locally defined period and retain a tested manual fallback. If no fallback is available, establish and test one before production restoration or make a separate, authorized degraded-mode decision. Reintroduce one permission or data source at a time; verify target logs and service view after each change. If a denied request succeeds, attribution is lost, NOC visibility degrades or new evidence widens the suspected path, return to the last verified state and reassess. The authorized service executive decides within delegation whether continued manual operation or the bounded restoration better fits service tolerance; escalate beyond appetite or authority. Estimate investigation, supplier support, NOC backfill and staged testing from local evidence.

## Use automation where its impact is limited

CISA’s August 2026 Logging Reference Architecture (LRA) targets U.S. Federal Civilian Executive Branch (FCEB) agencies; it is not a telco-specific standard. It says AI-assisted techniques can support incident triage, scoping, timeline reconstruction and summarization. Actions with material operational, legal or privacy consequences should remain subject to human review and established incident criteria. For an operator, use AI to assemble incident context and evidence, not to make a network-impacting decision on its own.

For the NOC assistant, the choice is a read/draft summary or manual review. Use the summary only when the operator can verify its source trail. Treat it as a map to evidence, not evidence itself. For claims that change severity or scope, require timestamps and references to source events; the NOC/SOC reviewer checks them before containment. If the assistant cannot show its inputs and identify missing feeds, or if it blends hypotheses with recorded events, use the manual path. That costs staff time, but a fluent summary that hides uncertainty can cost more. In an exercise, test traceability, missing-input visibility and continuity when the assistant is unavailable. Passing supports read/draft use; it does not authorize a network change.

Limited automation may support containment when its authority is narrow, its effect reversible and its actions recorded. Automatic revocation is a candidate only for a short-lived agent identity that does not support essential monitoring and whose downstream sessions have been tested. With shared credentials, the same action may remove visibility operators need to assess the incident. Define which actions are pre-authorized and the service conditions and delegated authority that bound them.

Prepare for short response windows without waiting to prove that an intrusion used AI. Plan for what an attacker can do, how quickly it may act and which services must keep running.

## The question for leadership

Executives should fund on-call coverage, supplier access and exercises, then confirm service tolerances and escalation limits. They should not approve each operational stop.

These decisions depend on the service, not a generic organization chart. If ownership or the safe next step is unclear, teams will debate roles and impact while the incident unfolds. Containment without service context can create a second service incident.

The response plan should name the path to stop, who can stop it and which service view must remain. Exercise that route with the on-call teams and suppliers who will use it; a role on a chart is not enough.
