# Chapter 5: Board Decisions Before the Next Incident

Before the next incident, the board needs a business decision, not a technology pitch. It must choose which service risk to reduce first, what work fits engineering and change capacity, and who owns the remaining risk while services continue. Directors need the likely service and customer impact. Management should show what is known and unknown, what evidence supports each option, and how it will reduce exposure over the next twelve months.

Chapter 4 set out the operating moves. This chapter turns their evidence into choices for the CEO and board.

## Speak in service-provider consequences

Board conversations are more effective when they begin with the service, not the tool. Depending on the operator and market, services may support emergency communications, payment services, public agencies, hospitals, enterprises and consumers. The UK Cyber Governance Code notes that cyber incidents can disrupt business continuity and damage customer trust.
Network criticality alone does not prove national impact. Describe affected services, their dependencies and likely consequences; state where evidence is incomplete.

State the service at stake, relevant workflow, current access, proposed action and remaining exposure. Mark missing facts and the evidence needed to close each gap; do not dramatize uncertainty.

A generic statement such as “AI is a major new threat” gives the board no decision. The NOC assistant described in Chapter 1 links alarm summaries to neighboring-domain data, supplier cases and NOC visibility. The board question is whether to protect capacity for a safer authority boundary, not which model to buy.

That choice has a capacity cost. Identity separation uses engineering and supplier time that may displace other network work; leaving capacity unprotected maintains broader access. The board can protect delivery capacity; management applies the authority model below to any continuing exposure.

If the operator relies on a legacy management platform, an external supplier or a narrow maintenance window, explain how that changes the response plan. A broad statement about “the network” is less useful than a clear account of which domain, service and recovery path are involved.

Chapter 4’s supplier-feature review gives the board a focused test: what changes when the model provider, data access, retention, processing location or supplier access changes? Can the operator verify or restrict each change without disrupting a needed service? Separate operator-tested boundaries from supplier assurances, and state the service trade-off where notice or restriction is unavailable.

**Distinguish feature disablement from supplier exit.** Turning off an AI feature may leave the surrounding management product and supplier support in place; ending the contract may still leave no tested way to continue service. Ask management which dependency the proposed work addresses and what continuity evidence remains open. Chapter 4 owns the operational feature-off and release gates; the board protects portability or continuity capacity when supplier dependency makes that work material.

## Show why the board is part of the operating model

The board sets risk appetite, resources and assurance expectations. It does not select a model, approve a firewall rule or accept operational service risk. Management owns those choices. The service owner supplies impact, tolerance, dependencies and fallback evidence; Security, Engineering, Procurement and Legal provide threat, technical, supplier, cost and regulatory evidence as relevant. The service executive with delegated authority across all affected services accepts residual service risk only within approved appetite. If no such authority exists, or exposure is outside appetite, escalate rather than treating it as accepted.

**One appetite, service-specific thresholds.** An enterprise appetite gives the board a consistent basis for funding and challenge, but one threshold can hide different service consequences. Revoking the NOC assistant’s account may remove alarm visibility; delaying a live-core patch leaves exposure while a supported change is prepared. A separate appetite for every product or platform creates parallel rules, incomparable reporting and repeated approvals. The useful choice is one enterprise appetite translated into a small, service-specific set of impact and escalation thresholds.

Use the same board reporting fields across services, but let each service owner supply its dependencies, tolerable impact and fallback. For the shared edge/core account, show which domains and operational views a restriction would affect. For the deferred patch, show which interim controls and service conditions make waiting tolerable, and which change requires escalation. A single “high” label cannot answer either question. Keep the authority model above: directors set appetite, resources and assurance; management makes service decisions within its delegation rather than sending every operational recheck to the board.

Building this comparison takes service mapping and owner time, but lets leaders compare priorities without claiming that service impacts are uniform. Each threshold should name a measurable impact and observable fallback, not only a “high/medium/low” label. If evidence is insufficient to define one, report the gap and fund the mapping rather than quietly treating it as tolerance.

Routine reporting and event-driven review do different jobs. The agreed board cadence shows trends, funding progress and assurance; it should not be the only route for a material change. A supplier changes retention or access, a revocation test fails, a safe window slips or a service fallback changes: each can make the previous decision stale. Define the signal that reopens the decision, the evidence management needs and the threshold for board escalation. If every technical change goes straight to directors, oversight becomes operational approval. If all changes wait for the next board pack, management may rely on an outdated boundary. Set the response interval from service tolerance and delegated authority, not a universal notification clock. Report exceptions through the normal board cycle unless they change appetite, resources or assurance sooner.

Funding one priority sets a sequence; it does not settle the exposures deferred to a later cycle. Management should show which services remain affected, what treatment is feasible and when the evidence or service conditions require a new decision.

The UK Cyber Governance Code calls for senior ownership of cyber risk, integration with enterprise risk management, a defined risk appetite and assurance that resources address those risks. It also recommends formal reporting at least quarterly with metrics and tolerances, and at least annual exercises with relevant internal and external stakeholders.
It is UK guidance, not a universal legal requirement. Its value here is the operating principle: cyber risk belongs in existing governance, with visible owners and a reporting cadence.

**Choose where board oversight sits.** A separate AI committee gives directors dedicated time, but adds an agenda and can divide attention from cyber, network resilience, procurement and enterprise risk. Folding the work into an existing cyber or risk committee connects it to service resilience; it can also bury agent-enabled workflows if the agenda, expertise or reporting is weak. A standing agenda item is often the least costly test, but only if directors receive service-level evidence rather than product demonstrations or a count of tools.

Before creating a new committee, ask whether the existing body can challenge the operator’s highest-impact uses: service and supplier dependencies, identity/data/action reach, observed control tests, fallback, material changes, open exceptions, resource displacement and overdue corrective actions. If the evidence is missing, improve the reporting and ownership first; another committee cannot repair an absent inventory or untested control. If the existing committee repeatedly lacks time or relevant network, supplier, operational or legal expertise, or a binding duty requires a separate structure, a dedicated committee may be justified. Its charter should state which decisions it oversees, what it reports to the full board, who supplies service evidence and where operational acceptance remains. It must not become a product-approval body or substitute for the delegated service executive.

Compare director and secretariat time, external expertise, reporting duplication and the risk that a new forum delays decisions against the value of focused challenge. Start within existing governance when it can surface material service risks and close actions. Add a standing agenda or separate committee when evidence shows a persistent oversight gap, material workload or an applicable duty requires it; verify that the new forum improves decision quality rather than moving the same unresolved items.

An official summary from the Australian Cyber Security Centre and the Australian Institute of Company Directors asks whether frontier AI changes attack-speed assumptions and supplier risk. It also asks whether an operator could keep its most important services running during a serious incident. This is cross-sector guidance, not telco-specific law or evidence of an operator incident. For a service provider, translate those questions into named services, supplier paths and tested fallback.

CISA’s Cross-Sector Cybersecurity Performance Goals 2.0, released in December 2025 for critical-infrastructure owners and operators, adds governance, accountability and risk management to its measurable, outcome-oriented baseline.
CISA describes the goals as a baseline for guiding investment, benchmarking progress and reducing risk measurably across IT and OT environments.
That is a useful way to frame the ask: fund outcomes and evidence, not a collection of AI-branded products.

For a multinational operator, prepare a short view by market: the duties that apply, reporting thresholds, accountable owners and dates. Separate binding requirements from consultations or emerging guidance. TRAI’s October 2026 AI guidelines are a draft consultation, not a final rule.
The draft offers a telecom-specific governance signal: it proposes board approval of an internal AI policy and a board-approved committee to oversee implementation for covered entities. That proposal is not a current obligation for every operator. Use it to test whether the existing governance body can see AI-enabled service risk; do not create a new committee solely because a consultation proposes one.
Describe something as a regulatory requirement only when it is in force and applies to the entity. Directors need to know what is binding, what is changing, and who is tracking the difference.

**Choose a market operating model before one assistant crosses borders.** Educational assumption: an operator wants one centrally managed NOC assistant to support teams in several markets, and local data, retention or approval rules may differ. This is a planning case, not a claim about a particular operator’s obligations. Three designs are available. A single global instance is operationally simple, but common configuration and broad identity can make market boundaries difficult to prove. Separate market instances provide clearer isolation, but duplicate engineering, support and assurance work and can fragment a shared NOC. A shared platform with market-specific identity, data routing, retention and action policy can balance those concerns, but only if those distinctions are enforced at the connectors and target systems, not merely written into prompts or administrative guidance.

The recommendation under this assumption is to use the shared model only after local Legal/Compliance confirm the applicable rules and technical owners demonstrate that identity, data and action boundaries hold per market. If the assistant or target cannot reliably distinguish the market of a request, exclude that market’s restricted data and actions or use a separate instance until the boundary is proven. Do not apply a single “strictest market” setting as a substitute for understanding where data may be processed or which local authority must approve an operational change. Conversely, do not let the convenience of a regional rollout override an unresolved market-specific duty.

The board’s decision is to fund the architecture and local review needed to make the model supportable, or to accept a narrower rollout with market exclusions. It does not accept service risk for each market on behalf of local delegated executives. Management brings back the boundary evidence and the service owners’ impact and fallback decisions before enabling a market; if the evidence fails, that market remains out of scope.

## Bring evidence the board can test

A board paper needs enough detail to support challenge, without making directors reconstruct the whole architecture. For each move, show the evidence and the decision it is meant to support.

The starting point is the set of AI-enabled workflows with the greatest potential service impact. For each, the paper can show what it can read, change or trigger, who owns it, which supplier and identity are involved, what is logged, what service is affected and who can suspend it. Gaps should be reported as gaps, not as green status.

Where a consequential service decision depends on joined operational records, Chapter 2’s source-contract check can provide one narrow evidence point: whether sources, freshness and conflicts are surfaced in the output. Report its scope and limits. It does not prove the identity boundary, tool restrictions or the agent’s general accuracy.

The access review should make authority visible: which priority identities are separate from shared accounts, which tools are read-only, which actions remain in normal network change control, and whether revocation has been tested. A count of AI systems cannot show whether their authority is controlled.

Exercise results add another kind of evidence. Did the SOC know who could revoke the identity? Did the NOC know what service might lose visibility? Could the operator move to an alternative mode? Were legal, customer and supplier contacts available? Report which decisions were unclear and whether owners closed the gaps. A June 2026 cross-sector statement by Five Eyes cyber-agency leaders says boards should ensure controls work under incident pressure, not merely exist. An exercise without corrective action is an event, not assurance.

Chapter 4 defines the evidence levels. For each board claim, name the level, service path and limitation, including whether visibility and fallback were covered. If a live integration cannot be tested safely, report the boundary that remains unknown and the decision it constrains; do not label it passed.

**Choose the assurance model to match the evidence gap.** Supplier material can describe intended behavior or controls the provider operates, but it cannot by itself show the operator’s deployed permissions or target result. Management-led testing sees the service context and the real dependency path, but uses scarce engineering and NOC time and may not challenge its own assumptions. Independent review can test whether the scope, evidence chain and conclusions withstand challenge; it is more expensive, depends on supplier access and still cannot prove behavior that the reviewer never observed.

For a high-impact workflow with a shared identity or supplier-managed hop, management can request funding for independent challenge of the test design and results while the operator retains control of the test, service fallback and risk decision. The board can protect assurance capacity without selecting operational tests. Ask the reviewer to examine the path from agent identity through connector to target, the allowed and denied requests, revocation, logs and service impact. The NOC and service owner confirm whether the test preserved the required operational view. For a low-impact read-only task with inspectable targets and clear ownership, management review may be proportionate; the board need not fund an external assessment for every feature.

Price the reviewer, supplier participation, engineering effort, safe test environment or window, and NOC backfill before setting the scope. If production testing is unsafe or a supplier will not expose a managed hop, an independent document review can improve confidence in the design but cannot prove runtime enforcement. Keep the deployment restricted until operator-verifiable evidence or an effective alternative boundary exists. Recommission assurance when a material change, failed test or service dependency alters the claim the board relies on.

Security work competes for engineers, maintenance windows and supplier capacity. If a control must wait for a platform upgrade, show the interim measure, its tested effect and the next decision trigger. CISA and the Canadian Centre for Cyber Security’s guidance on metered AI services notes that token consumption can affect cost, performance and availability. For a critical workflow, include the fallback if service degrades or becomes unavailable. If a regulatory deadline changes the sequence, show its date and tracking lead.

The dashboard must distinguish work in progress, a control tested effective, exposure accepted under defined conditions, and unresolved exposure without a valid decision. The worked dashboard keeps these states distinct; an owner or completion marker alone is not proof of control effectiveness.

## Ask for decisions, not general support

**Portfolio choice when capacity cannot fund everything.** For an educational planning exercise, assume the operator can protect two substantial technical work packages and one cross-functional exercise in the next cycle; the composites establish no such capacity limit or budget. The choice is not to pick the most visible AI product. It is to fund the evidence and changes that can reduce the greatest service exposure without using an unsafe window.

Use the same decision fields for all four composites:

- **Shared edge/core account:** service owner maps dependencies and tolerance; IAM, Engineering and the supplier test target permissions, attribution, revocation and fallback. The known reach makes this a concrete path to assess, but no production split is authorized by the scenario.
- **Supplier diagnostic feature:** prove whether disablement is independent, what identity/data/actions the feature uses, and whether the ordinary product remains supportable. Keep the feature restricted until the path and fallback are evidenced; do not infer direct execution from a proposal.
- **Deferred live-core patch:** first verify the deployed version, reachable exposure, interim controls and earliest supported change/rollback. A public exploitation signal can justify faster verification and readiness; it does not prove the operator is affected or make an unsupported change safe.
- **NOC assistant:** keep read/draft scope separate from action rights. Fund a bounded value comparison only when the identity, data and target boundary can be tested; no time-saving or quality result is established.

If only two technical packages fit, the service owners’ impact and tolerance evidence should determine which of these proceeds to implementation. A sensible first tranche may protect the edge-account boundary test and establish local patch applicability, while a smaller evidence request determines whether the supplier feature can be separated; the NOC value pilot remains bounded until authority is proven. This is a conditional sequence, not a universal ranking: a reachable, high-impact patch path or a failed containment test can change it. Reserve the cross-functional exercise for the actions that would affect service, and include NOC/SOC backfill and supplier participation in the estimate. For each selected outcome, management names an executive sponsor and operational lead, defines the evidence gate, escalation trigger and review date. The board can approve three or four outcomes and protect delivery and assurance capacity; management sequences the work and accepts operational risk only within delegation. Report what moves if local evidence changes the order. No cash costs, comparative risk scores or operator test outcomes are supplied for this exercise.

**Check shared dependencies before comparing package estimates.** The four composites can compete for some of the same scarce inputs: platform and identity engineers, supplier specialists, NOC backfill and supported maintenance windows. The case does not establish that the same suppliers or people support every workstream. Management should therefore show which estimates can run in parallel, which rely on a common supplier or window, and which smaller evidence task unlocks a later implementation decision. A low-cost mapping or feasibility task may be worth funding first if it determines whether a larger change is supportable; it is not automatically the highest risk-reduction item.

There are two common mistakes. Treat every work package as independent and promise all four for one cycle; shared staff or change windows make the plan impossible and hide displacement. Or rank the packages by a single risk score and fund the top item without asking whether the supplier path, fallback or test window exists. A better portfolio view separates readiness work from implementation, shows the constrained dependency and what can proceed while it is resolved, and names the decision that follows each evidence gate. If one package slips, move capacity only after checking that the alternative is ready and that the original exposure has a documented interim treatment; releasing a work slot does not accept or reduce that exposure.

For the shared account and patch, if local evidence shows both require the same supplier window, management compares combining them with separate windows under Chapter 4’s fault-isolation and rollback conditions. If either cannot be safely tested with the other, keep them separate even when capacity is tight. For the NOC assistant, a pilot can continue in a bounded mode while an unrelated patch proceeds only if they do not share the same service or operational capacity; do not assume parallel work is free. The board’s useful challenge is which dependency is the real bottleneck, what funded step removes uncertainty, and which service risk remains while the work waits.

**Fund the bottleneck revealed by the exercise.** For an educational board discussion based on the NOC assistant, assume an off-hours exercise found that responders waited because the containment trigger and deputy authority were unclear, while NOC staff were already handling manual alarm work. This is a decision assumption, not a reported exercise result. The board is choosing between additional on-call and backfill, supplier standby, more automation, or no new capacity.

On-call coverage and backfill can reduce the wait for a qualified operator, but create recurring staffing cost and may displace maintenance or other service work. Supplier standby brings platform knowledge when the supplier is the bottleneck, but adds contract cost and dependence; it does not establish that the supplier identity or target path is safe. Broader automation may reduce execution delay, but needs engineering and testing and can remove service visibility if the stop is too broad. No new spend preserves the current budget but leaves the response gap in place. These options solve different causes; funding the wrong one buys activity, not a faster safe decision.

Under the assumption above, protect a named deputy and NOC backfill, and revise the pre-authorized trigger before buying broader automation. Keep any automated stop narrow and limited to a path already tested at the target. Add supplier standby only if exercise evidence shows the delay depends on supplier availability or expertise. Management should provide local cost and displacement estimates; no cash figure or response improvement is established here.

Make the next exercise a funding check. Test the same service decision off-hours with the named deputy, with and without supplier participation, and record which interval improved, what service view was lost and how much manual work moved. If time remains in technical execution after authority and coverage are fixed, fund the relevant engineering or automation test. If the delay remains an approval question, do not solve it with more staff or a wider agent permission. The board protects resources and challenges the evidence; management keeps operational stop authority within its delegation.

The comparison should include the strongest feasible alternative. Retaining the shared edge/core account preserves the current arrangement but retains cross-domain reach; separation needs a supplier change and safe window. Show service impact, fallback and displaced work under both paths. If the reach crosses service domains, route the decision to an executive whose delegation covers the full impact; technical confirmation of reach does not confer service-risk authority.

The investment case should state what the money buys. It cannot guarantee that an incident will not happen. It should improve the operator’s ability to identify consequential uses, restrict unnecessary authority, contain an incident while maintaining service through a tested fallback, meet applicable duties and restore services. Some costs are people and change capacity, not software. Directors should understand both the risk the plan is meant to reduce and the work it will displace.

If Finance and Operations can substantiate a loss range, show the assumptions. If they cannot, explain service impact, response dependency and regulatory exposure without an unsupported monetary value. The decision remains real: fund the work now, choose another sequence or resource an interim treatment.

**Use an impact chain when a dollar range is not supportable.** A board can still make a disciplined choice if management separates what is confirmed from what is inferred and unknown. Begin with the service and its dependency: what service could be affected, which identity, supplier, workflow or maintenance window creates the path, and what operator decision would be delayed or made with less information. Then show the service owner’s tolerance and fallback, the evidence that supports each link, and the specific uncertainty a proposed work package will resolve. Do not assign a monetary value to an outage duration or customer consequence unless Finance and Operations can explain the method and assumptions.

This is more useful than a single “high” label. For the supplier feature, the decision may be to fund state confirmation and a supported disablement path before making a claim about exposure. For the NOC assistant, the evidence may show that review capacity and source coverage, rather than model quality, constrain safe use. For the deferred patch, version and reach evidence determine whether urgency can be ranked; an unknown version is a funded discovery task, not proof of either safety or immediate compromise. In each case, label the evidence owner and next decision date. The board can then compare the known service consequence, remaining uncertainty and displaced work without pretending the uncertainty has been converted into a precise loss estimate.

**Fund the control after the project closes.** A one-time project budget can pay for a new identity, a boundary test or a pilot. It does not pay to keep the result effective. Ask for separate estimates for delivery and recurring operation, with an owner for each. This prevents a control from being presented as complete when its identity reviews, logging, supplier coordination, fallback or staff coverage have no lasting budget.

There are three common funding homes. A central transformation fund can move early work quickly and avoid making one service absorb all setup costs; it can also leave the run team with a control it did not plan to operate. A service budget ties costs to the owner who receives the benefit and bears the impact, but may be difficult when the same identity or platform supports several services. A supplier contract can include support, notice or assurance work; it may reduce ad hoc effort, but does not replace operator verification or a fallback if the supplier is unavailable. The right mix depends on who controls the work and who must sustain the evidence.

The four composites illustrate different recurring costs. Shared-account separation needs ongoing identity ownership, review of access changes and supplier coordination after the initial migration. A supplier diagnostic feature needs release-change monitoring, evidence of the feature state and a manual diagnostic path if disabled. A NOC assistant needs source and connector maintenance, reviewer capacity, shift coverage, and reassessment when the model or workflow changes. A deferred patch requires version and reach tracking, interim-control checks and a supported window; the readiness reserve itself is not a permanent substitute for remediation. Estimate these from the operator’s actual process, not a generic AI percentage.

Before moving a capability into routine service ownership, name the run-budget owner, operational lead, technical evidence owner and review condition. If the recurring work is unfunded, narrow the deployment to what the existing team can support, keep it in a bounded pilot, or defer the handoff. The board can fund the initial and recurring capacity and challenge the estimate; management remains accountable for the service decision and the evidence that the control continues to work.

### Stage funding without approving the exposure

When the supplier cost, engineering effort or safe test window is still unknown, avoid presenting one fixed budget as if the delivery path were settled. The board can fund design and test capacity first, including supplier time and NOC backfill, while keeping authority at its current or more restricted level. Management then returns with the tested boundary, service impact, rollback path and an implementation estimate before asking for deployment funding.

This staged route limits the chance of funding a design that cannot be deployed safely, but it requires another decision and may delay the diagnostic benefit. Full funding at once can reserve scarce supplier or engineering capacity, but commits before the evidence is complete. Withholding all funds avoids new spend and leaves the current exposure and manual workload in place. Where current reach creates a material service concern, do not wait for the next budget decision to apply a safe interim restriction. Release the larger implementation budget only when local testing shows what the change permits, denies and does to service visibility; if the test fails, return with a safer restriction or a revised delivery path.

**Re-open the funding decision when discovery changes the scope.** Educational assumption: mapping a shared account or supplier path identifies additional affected services, or the bounded NOC pilot needs more review capacity than planned. These are plausible planning conditions, not reported findings. Management should not describe the resulting estimate change as either a project failure or a reason to fund everything automatically. It should return with the new dependency, which service tolerances are implicated, the estimate basis, and the work displaced by each route.

There are three defensible choices. Continue the full scope with additional supplier, engineering or NOC capacity; this preserves the intended outcome but requires the board to make the new resource trade-off explicit. Retain the work that closes the highest-consequence verified boundary and defer lower-priority scope; this protects the most important path sooner but leaves named gaps that require an interim treatment and review date. Pause the affected work while retaining only safe, funded containment or manual operation; this avoids spending against an unsupported design but may leave exposure open longer. The CISO should not treat an unapproved estimate increase as delegated risk acceptance, and management should not hide scope reduction inside a green delivery status.

If evidence narrows the path or removes the need for an expedited window, reduce or release the related reserve rather than spending it because it is available. If evidence expands the affected services or shows that the interim treatment is outside tolerance, ask for the resources needed to reduce the exposure and escalate any risk outside delegated authority. In either direction, distinguish project delivery from service-risk disposition: a smaller project can be the correct choice only if the remaining exposure has a named owner, a defensible treatment and an explicit return condition.

**Set the release condition for reserved capacity.** Design and test funding can expire without the technical problem being solved. If the supplier misses a test window or the service evidence is not ready, leaving the entire reserve untouched may block other work; releasing it immediately may make a supported window difficult to regain. Treat the reserve as a decision with its own owner, review point and opportunity cost, not as money that must be spent because it was approved.

At the review point, compare three choices. Keep the full reserve when a specific, supported decision window is still near and rebooking would materially delay a necessary test or safe change. Release it when evidence has removed the need for an expedited route or when the dependency has no credible date; do not retain a placeholder reservation simply to protect the appearance of progress. Hold only the capacity needed for the next verified step and return the balance to the portfolio when the remaining work is not yet decision-ready. In each case, name what work is displaced or restored and what service-risk treatment continues.

A failed gate is not a passed control and does not justify the original implementation spend. If the shared-account test cannot show a safe split, redirect the next tranche only to a tested restriction, supplier-supported alternative or service mapping needed for a new decision. If the NOC-assistant boundary fails, do not release scale-up funding; consider corrective testing or manual backfill. If patch evidence shows the local path is not exposed, release expedite capacity while keeping the ordinary patch process. If a supplier feature cannot be independently disabled, cost the supported pause, rollback or assurance route before committing to a wider rollout. These are conditional examples, not case outcomes.

The board can ask for the reserve to be released or redirected when the gate changes. Management must still decide whether current exposure remains within service tolerance and appetite; freeing budget does not close that risk. If the evidence is missing because a supplier or service owner has not delivered, identify the dependency and make a new choice rather than silently rolling the reservation and exception forward.

Apply staged funding to the shared edge/core account by protecting service-mapping and test capacity, not by presuming that a production cutover is ready. Until a target-side restriction is verified, the existing cross-domain reach remains. The board decision below sets the funding gate and the evidence management must return with.

## Board decision: shared edge/core account

The established fact is material: one account reaches edge nodes and parts of the core. The service dependencies, target permissions, attribution quality, safe migration method, rollback behavior and cost are not established. The board should not read “migration planned” as “reach reduced.” The operational result remains open until the target proves the new scope and the NOC confirms required views and workflows.

**Decision sought.** Reserve capacity for the service map, supplier-supported design and bounded test. Do not approve a production cutover or accept the account’s operational risk on the board’s behalf. The delegated service executive decides any continuing exposure within appetite and authority; management returns for additional implementation funding when the evidence is ready.

| Funding choice | What it buys and what remains | Board condition |
|---|---|---|
| Leave capacity unreserved | Avoids displacing current work, but delays dependency mapping and may leave the broad account in place longer. | Require an accountable executive to state whether the current reach remains within service tolerance and when the deferral is reviewed. |
| Fund mapping and test first | Buys a decision on target-native identity, an enforceable interim boundary, supplier support, service fallback and an estimate. It does not itself change access. | Release the design/test tranche now; require service-owner evidence, target-side allowed/denied results, session/revocation behavior and a rollback that exposes any return to broad access. |
| Fund the cutover before evidence | May reserve scarce supplier and engineering capacity, but commits to a path whose dependencies and service effect are unknown. | Do not choose on the current record. Reconsider only when support, safe window, fallback, test scope and effort are evidenced. |

The preferred sequence is therefore a funded design and test followed by a separate implementation decision. If target-native identities are supported and preserve service workflows, management can prepare that migration under normal change authority. If they are not available, an operator-controlled interim gateway is a candidate only if the test proves that all relevant routes traverse it, the target denies excluded access, logs identify the principal, revocation ends active access and gateway failure does not create an invisible bypass. These are release conditions, not claims that the gateway exists or has passed.

Management should return with a service-owner map, exact identity and target paths, supplier commitments, test evidence, rollback/fallback, implementation and recurring effort, and displaced work. A passed boundary test supports only the tested workflow. If safe separation fails, if essential NOC visibility would be lost, or if the supplier cannot support a safe window, the delegated executive must decide whether the current reach remains tolerable or whether a narrower interim treatment is required. Escalate beyond that executive’s authority; the board’s role is to resolve the resource or appetite question, not to order the technical cutover. Reopen the funding decision if reach, supplier support, service dependency or fallback changes.

**When the shared path crosses different service tolerances.** This is an educational decision variant, not a fact established by the composite: assume dependency mapping finds two affected services with different owners, fallback options and tolerable interruption, while the same shared account can reach both. The owners can each explain their own service impact; neither can accept the other service’s exposure. A low-impact result for one domain does not make the shared path acceptable for the second.

Ask management to present one row per affected service: dependency on the account, consequence of misuse or restriction, fallback, local tolerance and evidence owner. Then identify the executive whose delegation covers the combined reach. If one executive has that authority, the decision can be coordinated once while retaining separate service conditions. If authority is split, use the established executive escalation path to reconcile the decisions; do not average incompatible service tolerances or let the most tolerant owner speak for all affected services. If no delegated executive covers the combined impact, the current record has no valid acceptance decision. Management must seek an authorized interim restriction or escalate the authority question; the board should not fill the gap by approving operational exposure.

This mapping and joint review consume service-owner and engineering time, but can prevent duplicated testing and a false “approved” status. The board can fund that work and require a clear statement of who decides if the services disagree. Until the path is separated and verified, any continued use remains a shared exposure across the mapped services, not a set of independent permissions. Reopen the decision if a new service is found, a dependency changes, or a fallback used to justify the tolerance is not available when tested.

## Board decision: supplier diagnostic feature

The composite establishes that a routine product update enabled a supplier AI diagnostic feature that reads topology and proposes actions; direct execution is not established. Chapter 4 holds the worked operational release decision and its educational assumptions. No operator test result, measured benefit, cost or service outcome is a case fact.

**Decision sought.** Protect a bounded tranche for supplier data-path and support-access evidence, a feature-off/target-boundary test, NOC continuity and an estimate. This funds a decision, not enablement. Management selects the operational route within the authority model above and returns with the service owner’s impact/fallback view, supplier commitments and local effort estimate.

If a supplier-managed hop cannot be inspected directly, fund the narrowest independent review or operator-controlled boundary test that can answer the material question. If neither route proves the relevant boundary, keep the feature restricted. Use the results to decide whether the dependency ends at the AI feature or extends to the management product and service-support path. ## Make an exception expire in practice

A review date and an access expiry do different jobs. A review date tells management when to reconsider a decision; it does not disable a permission. An access expiry should cause the target to refuse new requests and should address existing sessions or queued actions. If the target cannot enforce that behavior, do not describe the access as time-bounded. Conversely, an account can remain technically active under normal change control while a temporary service-risk decision reaches its review point. That does not authorize indefinite continuation: the delegated executive must renew, restrict or escalate it.

A sound exception records the service and reach, evidence supporting the current choice, known unknowns, interim treatment, accountable decision-maker, review date, and conditions that bring the decision forward. Renewal is a new decision, not a calendar task. Management should show what changed, why the treatment is still needed, what the service owner says about current impact and fallback, whether the control remains effective, and what work or supplier dependency prevents closure. Do not let a model, supplier renewal or project status automatically extend an exception.

For the shared edge/core account, tie review to both a planned migration gate and any change in mapped service reach or tolerance. If the supplier window slips, return before the existing decision’s review point with a supported alternative, interim-control evidence and delegated authority. Do not extend the exception merely because migration remains on a roadmap. For the deferred patch, the three-week window is a planning fact, not an expiry of exposure; a slipped window requires a fresh view of local applicability, reachable path, interim control and service tolerance. For the supplier diagnostic feature, recheck the independent off state after a material update and reopen the choice if the feature is re-enabled or the supplier path changes. For the NOC assistant, the end of a bounded pilot should stop or hold the pilot scope unless a new scale-up decision is supported; it must not silently convert read/draft testing into standing production authority.

If a review point arrives without the evidence needed to renew, the default is not automatic continuation. Use the previously authorized safe restriction or manual fallback if available; if that would interrupt service, escalate to an executive with authority over the full impact before the decision lapses. If no safe route or decision-maker exists, raise the resource and authority gap through governance rather than leaving an undocumented exception. The board may fund the work needed to close it and ask whether appetite or resources need adjustment; management remains responsible for the operational choice.

## Keep the case defensible

Compliance alone does not show that a service can recover, and a platform request must name the control gap. Do not promise “zero risk” or “future-proof.” Show what the investment changes, which dependencies remain and what management must still decide.
